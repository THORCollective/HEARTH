title: A raw packet socket with a classic BPF filter attached on a Linux appliance that has no business capturing traffic
hypothesis: >-
  A passive backdoor is resident on a Linux network-edge appliance — a mail gateway, firewall, DVR or NAS — holding an open `AF_PACKET`/`SOCK_RAW` socket with a classic BPF program attached via `setsockopt(SO_ATTACH_FILTER)`, so that it never listens on a TCP or UDP port and never appears in `netstat`, `ss` or an external port scan. Hunt for processes holding packet sockets with attached filters on hosts that run no legitimate packet-capture, IDS or network-monitoring tooling, and correlate each one against the process name it claims to be.

  Packet sockets with attached BPF filters are entirely normal on the right hosts. `tcpdump`, `dumpcap`, `wireshark`, `libpcap`-linked tooling, Suricata, Zeek, Snort, nmap, dhclient, `dhcpcd`, `wpa_supplicant`, `arpwatch`, `keepalived`, container and overlay networking agents, and most commercial network-monitoring and NDR sensors all open them legitimately.   Core filter. Enumerate every process holding an `AF_PACKET` socket, then subtract the host's legitimate capture and monitoring tooling. `/proc/net/packet` lists packet sockets with their inode; map inode to PID by walking `/proc/*/fd`. Triage fields - PID, the `exe` symlink target, `cmdline`, UID, process start time relative to host boot, and whether the kernel reports a filter attached.
  Sample triage commands. `cat /proc/net/packet` then `ls -l /proc/*/fd 2>/dev/null | grep socket` to map inodes. Or in one pass - `for p in /proc/[0-9]*; do if ls -l $p/fd 2>/dev/null | grep -q socket; then inode=$(ls -l $p/fd 2>/dev/null | grep -oE 'socket:\[[0-9]+\]' | grep -oE '[0-9]+'); for i in $inode; do grep -q " $i " /proc/net/packet 2>/dev/null && echo "PID ${p#/proc/} exe=$(readlink $p/exe) cmdline=$(tr '\0' ' ' < $p/cmdline)"; done; fi; done`. On a kernel with BPF introspection, `bpftool prog list` and `ss -0 -p` add corroboration.
  auditd rules that make this collectable. `-a always,exit -F arch=b64 -S socket -F a0=17 -k packet_socket` captures `AF_PACKET` socket creation (17 is `PF_PACKET`), and `-a always,exit -F arch=b64 -S setsockopt -k sockopt` captures filter attachment; `SO_ATTACH_FILTER` is option 26 and `SO_ATTACH_BPF` is 50, so filter on `a2` to cut volume. These rules fire at implant startup, which means they catch a reinfection or a reboot-relaunch rather than an implant already resident - so pair the live `/proc` sweep for what is running now with the audit rules for what starts next.

# H366

The reason this one is worth hunting is what it does *not* do.

A conventional backdoor binds a port. That gives a defender something to find: a listener in `ss -ltnp`, a service in an asset inventory, a response to an external scan. BPFDoor binds nothing. It opens a raw packet socket, attaches a small classic BPF program, and sleeps. Every packet on the wire gets evaluated by the kernel against that filter, and only when one carries the right magic value does the implant wake up and connect back, open a bind shell, or knock over UDP. Scan the appliance from outside and it looks exactly as it did the day before it was compromised.

Rapid7's October 2026 research tracked these across Linux network-edge devices in telecom environments: a new BPFDoor variant, a BPF Rekoobe build against South Korean targets, and six builds of an implant they call AVERAT on Taiwanese appliances. The filters were small — 16 and 26 BPF instructions — and the magic values varied per sample, including a 14-byte payload constant that was unique to each one.

What makes a hunt possible is that the socket itself is visible even when the port is not. `/proc/net/packet` lists every packet socket on the host. On a mail gateway or a DVR, that list should be short and boring, and every entry should trace back to a daemon the vendor shipped.

The second half is identity. These implants rewrite their own `argv` and call `prctl(PR_SET_NAME)` to claim names drawn from a table — `/usr/sbin/chronyd`, `/usr/sbin/rsyslogd -n`, `ora_ppmond`, and in two cases `[watchdogd]` and `[kaluad_sync]`, which are formatted to imitate kernel threads. A process in square brackets that has a real executable on disk is not a kernel thread. Comparing `cmdline` against the `exe` symlink costs nothing and defeats the entire disguise.

## Why

- **The technique is defined by the absence of a listener.** No bound port means no entry in `netstat`, no response to a scan, and nothing for an asset-inventory diff to notice. Port-centric detection is structurally blind to it, which is exactly why it is used on devices that get scanned from outside.
- **The socket is still enumerable.** `/proc/net/packet` is a complete list, and mapping its inodes to PIDs through `/proc/*/fd` turns an invisible implant into a row in a table. The hunt works because the kernel keeps the bookkeeping the implant tries to avoid.
- **The right hosts have almost no legitimate packet sockets.** A mail security gateway is not a capture host. Once the vendor's own daemons are allowlisted per device class, the remainder is small enough to look at by hand — which is what makes this tractable on appliances with no EDR.
- **The process names are a disguise that inverts into a signature.** Spoofing `chronyd` works on a reviewer reading a process list. It fails immediately against a comparison of `cmdline` to `exe`, and the `[bracketed]` names fail even faster.
- **Appliances are chosen because they are unmonitored.** Rapid7's samples were tailored per vendor — a SpamSniper PID file, a ShareTech-derived key, Oracle telecom daemon naming. The operator is relying on these devices sitting outside the estate's normal telemetry. Hunting them directly checks that assumption.
- **Lifetime separates the implant from the tool.** `tcpdump` and `dhclient` hold packet sockets briefly. An implant holds one for the host's entire uptime. Sorting the survivors by process start time relative to boot is a cheap second cut.
- **It scopes a reinfection, not just a compromise.** The auditd rules on `socket(AF_PACKET)` and `setsockopt(SO_ATTACH_FILTER)` fire when a new implant starts, which is the signal that the firmware's own package directory is relaunching it at boot.
