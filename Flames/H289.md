  legitimately report mismatches after an in-place upgrade, after a vendor hotfix, on hosts where OpenSSH
  was compiled from source or installed from a third-party repository, and on appliances and container
  images that ship a modified sshd by design. Enumerate those populations once and carry them as a
  documented exception list rather than re-triaging them monthly; note that `rpm -Va` also flags mode,

# H289

There is a category of credential access that never touches LSASS, never reads `/etc/shadow`, and never
generates a single failed authentication: you stop trying to recover the password and simply record it
being typed. On Linux the natural place to do that is `sshd`, and the natural place inside sshd is
`userauth_passwd()` — the function that holds the user's plaintext password for the moment between it
arriving off the wire and being passed to PAM.

Rapid7 documented exactly that in the DPRK activity targeting South Korean media and automotive
organizations. Alongside the Ted backdoor and CurlRAT, the operators deployed a modified `sshd` that
hooked `userauth_passwd()`, captured every username and password presented to the host, and appended
them to `/var/lib/sshd/c8c68e629bba773a10ac80012d10bf19`, encrypted with a custom substitution cipher
layered over Base64. Authentication proceeded normally afterwards. The build also carried the operators
own passwords hardcoded, and declined to log a credential that matched one of them — a small detail that
says the tooling was expected to sit in place long enough that self-collection was a real concern.

Three things about that make it worth a hunt rather than a signature.

The first is that the malicious behaviour has no user-visible symptom. Nothing fails, nothing slows,
no account locks out. Unlike a credential-dumping tool, which has to touch a protected process or a
protected file and can be caught doing it, this waits. Every administrator who logs into the host after
the replacement hands over a working credential, and on a jump host or a bastion that is the entire
administrative population.

The second is that the on-disk artifact is far easier to find than the technique suggests, because the
operators chose a directory that is supposed to be empty. `/var/lib/sshd`, `/var/empty/sshd` and
`/run/sshd` are privilege-separation chroots. Their emptiness is the point. A regular file sitting in one
of them is not a tuning problem, it is a finding — which is unusual enough in Linux hunting to be worth
building a standing check around, independent of this campaign.

The third is that the encryption defeats the obvious hunt. Searching hosts for plaintext credentials on
disk would have found nothing here. The durable observables are structural: a file where no file belongs,
`sshd` writing to something it has no business writing to, and an `openssh-server` package that no longer
verifies.

This is one of four behaviours from the same intrusion that HEARTH did not previously cover. [[H290]]
covers the timestomping the operators used on the daemons they replaced — relevant here because it means
the mtime on a replaced `sshd` will lie to you. [[H291]] covers the selective log erasure that removed
their traces from `auth.log` and `secure`, which is the same authentication surface this hunt watches.
[[B044]] baselines the shared objects that legitimately load into long-running daemons, which is the
companion problem when the implant is loaded rather than compiled in. Mandiant reported PAM manipulation
by BREEZE COMET in an unrelated financially-motivated campaign in the same period, so this is not a
single-actor concern.

## Why

- **It converts every future administrative login into a credential the operator owns.** Unlike a
  one-shot credential dump, a patched `sshd` keeps producing. On a bastion, a jump host, or any box the
  platform team touches daily, the yield after a week is the working password of everyone who
  administers the estate — including accounts that were never on the host at the time of compromise.
  Dwell time and damage scale together, which makes the detection latency here matter more than usual.
- **There is no failure signal to alert on.** The technique is invisible to every control that watches
  for authentication going wrong: no failed logons, no account lockouts, no anomalous privilege use, no
  new account. It succeeds by letting authentication work exactly as designed. That removes the entire
  authentication-anomaly detection class from play and leaves host integrity as the only place it can be
  caught.
- **The chosen hiding place is a directory that is supposed to be empty, which is a gift.** Most Linux
  hunting fights a noise floor. `/var/empty/sshd` and `/var/lib/sshd` have essentially none — they are
  privilege-separation chroots, and emptiness is their function. A standing check for regular files in
  them costs nothing to run, needs no baseline, and would have caught this campaign on the first pass.
  That check is worth deploying whether or not this specific actor is in scope.
- **Encryption of the credential log defeats the intuitive hunt and pushes you to the better one.**
  Grepping the estate for known passwords on disk would have returned clean. The operators used a custom
  substitution cipher plus Base64 specifically so that content-based searching fails. Hunting the
  structure instead — wrong file in the wrong directory, wrong process doing the writing, package that
  no longer verifies — is both what works here and what generalizes to the next implant with a different
  encoding.
- **Package verification is an underused, high-yield control on Linux estates.** Most organizations have
  file-integrity monitoring on Windows and nothing equivalent on Linux. `rpm -Va` and `debsums` are
  already installed, already know the vendor hashes, and answer the question directly. The work is not
  building the check, it is enumerating the legitimate exceptions once — source builds, appliances,
  third-party repositories — so the result is readable.
- **A replaced binary requires a restart, which puts a second event in the timeline.** The write alone
  can be argued about. A write to `/usr/sbin/sshd` followed within seconds by a service restart, outside
  any change window, cannot. Correlating the two turns a moderate-confidence integrity finding into a
  high-confidence one and gives the investigation an anchor timestamp that the timestomping in [[H290]]
  cannot erase.
