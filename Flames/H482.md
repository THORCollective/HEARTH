title: "Unauthenticated xml.cgi action calls against internet-exposed refrigeration controllers: unit enumeration, a date read before a date-derived authentication bypass, password spraying, and an account rename that needs no credentials"
hypothesis: >-
  Internet-exposed building and refrigeration controllers are attacked through their own XML API rather
  than through any code-execution flaw, and the whole sequence is visible in HTTP requests to one endpoint.
  Reconnaissance calls an action that enumerates manager units and their addresses, which on a configured
  controller also returns the identity and location of the site it serves. A read of the controller's own
  date is the prelude to an authentication bypass that compares the first five characters of the supplied
  password against a code derived from the current date, so a correct date makes a username unnecessary.
  Password spraying against the fixed administrative account names runs in parallel. A separate
  unauthenticated request renames an account by numeric identifier, which exists to make the login name
  predictable before a second unauthenticated request resets its password. The impact is not destruction
  but quiet parameter change: temperature setpoints, defrost cycles and the alarm thresholds that would
  have told maintenance staff something was wrong.

false_positive_notes: |
  These devices are administered remotely by several parties at once, so unauthenticated and automated access is routine and the discriminators are the action called and the pattern of calls.
  - Maintenance contractors, integrators and the equipment vendor all connect to these controllers remotely and legitimately, often from addresses the asset owner has never recorded. Enumerate who is supposed to connect and from where before treating an external source as hostile; on many sites the answer is that nobody has ever written it down, which is itself the finding to report.
  - The monitoring platform polls these controllers constantly. Regular reads of unit lists and device state are the normal traffic of a refrigeration estate. The pattern to hunt is a source that calls a read action across many controllers it has no relationship with, or that follows a read with authentication attempts, rather than a read on its own.
  - Internet-wide scanning and research projects probe these devices continuously. Sekoia itself could not determine whether some of the date reads it observed were hostile or researchers, and said so. Expect a steady background of unauthenticated reads from scanning infrastructure, and rank by what followed the read rather than by the read.
  - A failed authentication attempt against an administrative account is not evidence of compromise. These accounts have fixed names and are sprayed constantly. The finding is a success, or a spray followed by a configuration change, or a spray followed by an account rename.
  - Legitimate parameter changes happen daily. Setpoints are adjusted seasonally, defrost schedules are tuned, and alarm limits are widened on purpose when a site is generating nuisance alarms. Compare against change records and against the site maintenance schedule; the discriminator is an alarm threshold widened at the same time as a setpoint moved, which is a combination a technician has no reason to make.
  - Where no HTTP log exists on the controller, this hunt runs on the perimeter record instead and loses the action names. A flow-level finding - a single external source reaching many controllers - is weaker but still actionable, and on most estates it is the only evidence available.
notes: |
  Source reporting is Sekoia TDR's follow-up investigation, published after a private report of October 13 to 14 2026, into attacks against unpatched Danfoss refrigeration controllers. Sekoia reports that between 11 and 13 October 2026, "hundreds of supermarkets across France, including stores operated by Intermarché and Coopérative U running unpatched Danfoss refrigeration control systems were affected by a large-scale cyberattack", with a potential impact that "could reach tens of millions of euros, through food spoilage and business interruption."
  Why the attack is worth a hunt despite being unsophisticated - Sekoia is direct that "these two attack campaigns weren't sophisticated. The main issue exploited by the threat actor was the direct exposure of unpatched Danfoss controllers to the public Internet." That is the point: the capability required is low, the exposure is large - Sekoia observed via Censys "more than 2,700 Danfoss systems exposed worldwide as of October 11, including approximately 1,700 in France" - and the impact lands in a place nobody monitors. Sekoia reports that "the threat actor automatically modified temperature settings, defrost cycles, and alarm thresholds to evade detection by maintenance personnel for several hours", and that the actor "didn't exploit any code execution flaw, which prevented further damage against the controllers, such as wiping their file system."
  No existing HEARTH coverage - Grepped the corpus for refrigeration, controller API endpoints and setpoint manipulation and found nothing. HEARTH has no hunt covering building-automation or refrigeration control systems at all, which makes this the first in that space; the structural approach generalises past this vendor to any controller with an unauthenticated XML or CGI management API.
  Core filter 1, the endpoint and its actions - The hunt itself. Sekoia deployed four honeypots of Danfoss 800A web interfaces and reports that one "received unauthenticated HTTP requests attempting to enumerate System Manager units and their associated IP addresses via the read_units action", and that over following weeks it saw "multiple requests targeting the xml.cgi endpoint from 26 unique IP addresses. These requests ranged from reconnaissance activities using the read_units or read_date_time actions to password-spraying attempts targeting the Supervisor and admin accounts with the password 12345." Select every request to the controller's XML endpoint, group by source address, and report the set of actions each source called and whether any authentication attempt followed. Triage values are the action name, whether the request carried credentials, the response status, and the number of distinct controllers the same source touched.
  Core filter 2, the date read as a prelude - This is the detail that converts a boring reconnaissance log line into a high-priority one. Sekoia explains the bypass: the vulnerability "compares the first five chars of the password XML attribute against a digit code generated from the current date. If these chars match the generated code, authentication is bypassed even without providing a valid username, granting full access to the device's API endpoints." Because the code derives from the date, the actor needs the device's own date first, and Sekoia notes that "the requests made to the read_date_time action in our honeypot logs may be linked to the exploitation prelude of CVE-2025-41450, as threat actors would need to determine each system date before attempting to exploit it." Hunt for a date read followed within a short window by an authentication attempt from the same source against the same controller. Sekoia is careful about confidence here, noting that it "could not confirm whether this vulnerability was exploited during the first campaign" and that some date reads "may have originated from malicious threat actors or security researchers simply actively probing internet-exposed Danfoss instances."
  Core filter 3, the rename that needs no credentials - Sekoia reports one confirmed exploitation attempt against four honeypots on November 1 between 10:00 p.m. and 10:30 p.m. from 37.16.75[.]128, in which "a threat actor tried to exploit a non-public vulnerability aimed at renaming a user account". Working with a model and vendor firmware images, Sekoia established that "the captured request can allow a threat actor to rename any user account simply by providing its numerical identifier, without any authentication. In the exploit observed in the wild, the threat actor targeted user ID 1, an account with full administrative privileges." The purpose is sequencing, as Sekoia explains: "the threat actor needs to know the account's login - as a primitive - to reset its password through a second unauthenticated request, which was not captured because the first request was blocked. Since the login name can change, the attacker must first set it to a known value to ensure the password-reset exploit works reliably." So hunt the controller's user list for an account whose name is not the one your records say it should be. On an estate with hundreds of controllers that comparison is a script, and a renamed administrative account is unambiguous.
  Core filter 4, the impact, which nobody is watching - The hardest and most important check, because it is where the damage lives and because the device may log nothing. Compare scheduled configuration snapshots for changes to temperature setpoints, defrost schedules and alarm limits, and treat a simultaneous change to a setpoint and to the alarm threshold that governs it as the finding. A widened alarm threshold is the tell: a technician adjusting a setpoint has no reason to also raise the limit that would report the result. Pair this with the monitoring platform's own alarm record, because an absence of alarms after a threshold change is exactly what the actor was buying - Sekoia reports the changes were made "to evade detection by maintenance personnel for several hours."
  Sample estate-wide exposure check, to be run against the organisation's own external ranges and inventory rather than against the internet at large:
  ```bash
  set -u
  # expects a file of "host:port" for every controller the inventory knows about
  while IFS= read -r target; do
    host=${target%%:*}; port=${target##*:}
    code=$(curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}' --max-time 5 \
             "http://${host}:${port}/xml.cgi" 2>/dev/null || echo "000")
    printf '%s\t%s\t%s\n' "$host" "$port" "$code"
  done < controllers.txt
  ```
  Any controller that answers this at all from outside the network is the finding, independent of whether an attack has occurred, and the output doubles as the exposed-population list for the vendor advisories. The same loop run from inside and outside gives the difference between what is reachable internally and what is reachable from the internet, which on these estates is frequently not what anyone expects.
  Precedent, for why this target set is not an accident - Sekoia notes that "Iran-nexus threat actors have a well-documented track record of targeting Internet-facing industrial control systems", citing CyberAv3ngers compromising exposed Unitronics controllers at the Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa in late 2023 by exploiting default credentials, and a July 2026 coordinated disruption of over 30 Minnesota water systems. On refrigeration specifically, it records that "in 2022, approximately 40 Israeli supermarkets operating Danfoss controllers were compromised" and that "in late August 2026, at least six US military bases in mainland USA were impacted by refrigeration failures, with freezers forced into defrost mode in the same way observed in France", while being explicit that "no technical indicators currently link this incident to that cluster."
  Expected, not observed by Sekoia - The exact request shape of the rename and password-reset exploits. Sekoia describes what the captured request can do and why it is needed, having analysed it against vendor firmware, but deliberately does not publish the request, so filter 3 above is written against the outcome - a renamed administrative account - rather than against a request signature.
  Scope - Any organisation operating refrigeration, HVAC or building-automation controllers, which includes retail, food distribution, pharmaceutical storage, hospitals and data-centre cooling. Sekoia's closing point is the one that makes this an ownership problem rather than a technical one: "responsibility for these measures is often distributed across asset owners, integrators, maintenance providers and equipment vendors, but this division of responsibility should not result in persistent exposure." A hunt that produces nothing except an accurate inventory of which controllers answer from the internet has already paid for itself.

# H482

The freezers were set to defrost. The alarm thresholds were widened so nobody would be told.

Between 11 and 13 October 2026, hundreds of supermarkets across France lost their cold chain — Intermarché and Coopérative U stores among them — and the potential cost, through spoilage and business interruption, runs into tens of millions of euros. There are photographs of the discarded stock.

Sekoia's assessment of the attack is blunt: it was not sophisticated. No code execution, no implant, no custom tooling. The controllers were sitting on the public internet running old firmware, and the attacker used their XML API.

Three things make this worth a hunt rather than a news item.

First, the exposure is enormous and nobody owns it. Sekoia counted more than 2,700 of these controllers reachable from the internet, about 1,700 of them in France. These devices are installed by integrators, maintained by contractors, and monitored by a platform the retailer's security team has never logged into. The asset inventory, in most cases, does not exist.

Second, the authentication bypass is almost funny. The device compares the first five characters of the supplied password against a code generated from the current date. Get the date right and you do not need a username. Which is why a request asking the controller what time it thinks it is turns out to be the interesting log line — it is the step before the bypass.

Third, and this is the part that generalises: the attacker did not break the controllers. They changed the setpoints, changed the defrost cycles, and then changed the alarm thresholds that would have surfaced the result. The damage was done by the equipment, operating exactly as instructed, for several hours before anyone noticed.

A widened alarm limit is the artefact worth hunting. A technician adjusting a temperature has no reason to also raise the threshold that would report the consequences.

## Why

- **The hunt's first output is an asset inventory, and that alone justifies running it.** Most organizations cannot say how many refrigeration or building-automation controllers they operate, let alone which answer from the internet. A loop that asks each one whether it responds externally produces a list that no other process in the business produces.
- **The impact is designed to be unreported, so absence of alarms is not evidence of health.** Changing the alarm threshold alongside the setpoint means the monitoring platform stays quiet while the stock warms up. Any hunt that relies on the device or its platform to raise a problem is relying on the exact control the attacker adjusted.
- **A date read is a prelude, and reading it that way costs nothing.** On its own, a request for the controller's clock is the dullest line in the log. Paired with the knowledge that the bypass derives its code from the date, a date read followed by an authentication attempt from the same source becomes a specific, high-priority sequence — and the reinterpretation is free.
- **The administrative account names are fixed, so a renamed one is unambiguous.** The rename exists to make the login predictable before an unauthenticated password reset. That means the compromise leaves a comparison you can automate across an estate: the account names on the device versus the account names your records expect.
- **The capability bar is low and the precedent is long.** Exposed controllers with default credentials at a water authority in 2023, Israeli supermarkets in 2022, US military base freezers forced into defrost in August 2026. This is a repeatable playbook against a class of device that sits outside everyone's security programme, and the skill required to run it is dropping rather than rising.
