false_positive_notes: |
  The hard part of this hunt is not benign noise in the usual sense — it is that the malicious activity is performed by a tool whose entire job is to perform exactly that activity. An N-central administrator legitimately pushes scripts and jobs to many endpoints at once, legitimately opens Take Control sessions into domain controllers, and legitimately does so at odd hours during a maintenance window. There is no endpoint-side artefact that distinguishes an attacker-initiated job from an administrator-initiated one, which is precisely why the hunt is scoped to the appliance. On the appliance the discriminators are different in kind and much sharper. `getPierDetails` requests with sequentially enumerated appliance IDs are not a shape any console UI produces — a human operator navigates to specific devices, not through an ID range, so the benign population for enumeration is effectively empty and the tuning question is only how wide a window counts as sequential. 2xx responses to internal API routes carrying `%2F` are similarly rare - legitimate clients emit encoded slashes in a small, enumerable set of places, so one baselining pass over normal traffic produces a short allowlist and everything outside it is worth reading. Take Control sessions have a genuine benign population and the discriminator there is not the session but its context - the target (a domain controller versus a helpdesk workstation), the time (inside or outside a declared change window), and the initiating account. "MSP Support" is the DEFAULT Take Control account name, so in many environments it is also the legitimate one, and it must be treated as a pivot rather than as an indicator by itself; the useful version of that query is sessions from that account to targets it has no history of touching. The `.invalid` email mutation has no benign explanation at all - no administrator appends `.invalid` to their own address, and the whole point of the mutation is to break the account's ability to receive mail, so a single match is actionable on its own with no tuning. Two things make an empty result genuinely ambiguous here and both should be checked before it is recorded. First, appliance-side logging is limited and short-lived by default — Huntress was unable to determine definitively which exploit the actor used precisely because of limited historical logging on the appliance, so the absence of `getPierDetails` probing may simply mean the log rotated. Second, hosted (NCOD) instances are not under the customer's control for log retrieval at all, so for those the appliance surface is not available and the hunt degrades to the downstream pivot, which is the weaker half. Finally, the source article contains no detection queries of any kind, so every query below is constructed from the reported artefacts rather than reproduced from the vendor.

# H327

Huntress documented active in-the-wild exploitation of CVE-2026-86218, a CVSS 10.0 pre-authentication remote code execution flaw in the N-able N-central web tier, with a confirmed impacted customer. The vulnerability affects all current versions including 2026.3, hosted and on-premises, and sits alongside a cluster of companion CVEs — CVE-2026-86206 and CVE-2026-86207 (authentication bypass), CVE-2026-18556 and CVE-2026-18577. The N-central server runs a custom distribution of AlmaLinux 9, and that appliance is where this hunt lives. Before exploitation the actor probed `/remoteControlAction.do?method=getPierDetails` with enumerated appliance IDs to map the environment, and successful requests to internal API routes carried URL-encoded values such as `%2F`. Afterwards the RMM itself became the weapon: scripts and jobs pushed out to managed endpoints, and Take Control sessions opened into domain controllers and other critical systems, with process enumeration on compromised hosts. The actor also appended strings such as `.invalid` to known N-able administrator email addresses, locking the real administrators out of their own console. The appliance-side logs that matter are `envoy_proxy_HTTPS.log`, `syslog ncentraldms` and `ui_access_control.log`. The article contains no detection queries at all.

## Why

- **An RMM server is a force multiplier, and the endpoint is the wrong place to look.** One pre-auth RCE converts into authenticated, tool-sanctioned code execution on every managed endpoint in the estate. At those endpoints the execution is performed by a trusted agent under its normal service account, with an expected parent process, a signed binary and a normal network destination — there is no anomaly to detect, because the tool is doing exactly what it is designed to do. The intrusion is only cleanly separable from legitimate administration at the point where the instruction originated, which is the appliance.
- **The `.invalid` email mutation is free, instant and unambiguous.** No administrator ever appends `.invalid` to their own address; the whole purpose of the mutation is to break mail delivery so the real administrators cannot receive alerts or complete a password reset. It is a direct query against the N-central user store rather than a log search, it needs no tuning and no baseline, and it produces zero false positives by construction. It should be the first thing run against any N-central instance that was unpatched during the exposure window, ahead of all the log work.
- **Enumeration has a shape no console user produces.** `getPierDetails` requests walking a range of appliance IDs from a single source is not how a human operator navigates — they go to named devices they already care about. That makes the pre-exploitation reconnaissance detectable as a *pattern of requests* rather than as a signature, which matters because the pattern survives a change of endpoint, parameter name or payload while a signature does not.
- **Three consumer dynamic-DNS hostnames are better hunted as a class than as indicators.** All three reported C2 domains are Synology QuickConnect or synology.me addresses. Matching the three hostnames catches this actor once; querying for any `*.direct.quickconnect.to` or `*.synology.me` resolution from server infrastructure catches the next one too, and a management appliance reaching a consumer NAS dynamic-DNS provider is an inversion of its expected traffic profile that essentially never has a legitimate explanation.
- **Limited appliance logging means an empty result has to be interrogated, not trusted.** Huntress could not definitively determine which exploit was used because of the limited historical logging available on the appliance itself — the same constraint that will face anyone hunting this. Before recording a true negative, confirm the retention window of `envoy_proxy_HTTPS.log` actually covers the exposure period, and note that on hosted NCOD instances those logs are not customer-retrievable at all, which reduces the hunt to its weaker downstream half.
