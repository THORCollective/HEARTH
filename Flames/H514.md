false_positive_notes: |
  The sending and tracking infrastructure is shared and legitimate. Microsoft's own guidance is explicit - avoid broadly blocking shared email-delivery infrastructure based only on this campaign, and combine sender, content, URL and behavioural signals instead. An organisation that blocks a bulk email service provider's click-tracking host because it appears here will break its own marketing, transactional and notification mail. Use the provider path as a correlating signal, never as a sole indicator.

  Legitimate service-health and outage notifications exist, including from Microsoft and from third-party status services, and some are relayed through email service providers by managed service providers and resellers. The discriminator is the destination - Microsoft's own point is that legitimate Microsoft 365 service-health communications do not direct users to non-Microsoft domains to recover held mail. Build the hunt around vendor impersonation plus off-vendor destination, which is the durable property.

  The subject regular expression is tuned to one campaign and will not survive a change of token length or separator. Treat it as a cheap first pass and keep the structural version - a service-health or outage subject from an external sender whose links leave the vendor's own domain space - as the standing hunt.

  The three-character display-name prefix is a per-target personalisation, so a display name resembling a legitimate internal abbreviation is the intended effect rather than an accident. Reviewers will find some of these look plausible, and that is the point - do not dismiss a message because its display name resembles a known brand or a known internal team.

  Recipient-specific tracking URLs mean no two recipients received the same link, so deduplicating by URL understates the campaign's footprint and a URL-based block list will not cover it. Count by message and by recipient, not by distinct URL.

  A click is not a credential submission. Click telemetry records interaction; whether the page loaded and whether a password was typed are separate questions answered by proxy evidence and by subsequent identity telemetry. Equally, no click does not clear a user whose mail client prefetched links.

  Finally, Microsoft reports that messages in environments protected by Defender for Office were blocked or quarantined and that it observed no indicators of compromise from this attack. A clean result is therefore the likely and expected outcome in a protected tenant - which makes this hunt a verification exercise, and worth running precisely because the expected answer is nothing.
notes: |
  Leg one, find the messages by structure (EmailEvents) - Microsoft publishes the sender-domain and subject query directly, and the regular expression is the reusable part:

      let campaignDomains = dynamic([
          "moments-notice.com",
          "em4493.moments-notice.com",
          "newbooker.com",
          "em.newbooker.com"
      ]);
      EmailEvents
      | where SenderFromDomain in~ (campaignDomains)
          or Subject matches regex
              @"(?i)^(?:\[external\]\s*|\*external\*\s*)?outage\s*-\s*[a-z0-9]{6}$"
      | project Timestamp, NetworkMessageId, RecipientEmailAddress,
          SenderFromAddress, SenderFromDomain, SenderDisplayName,
          Subject, DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation
      | order by Timestamp desc

  Note the two alternatives are deliberately independent - the domain list is this campaign, the regular expression is the pattern. Microsoft's queries set no time range, so use the advanced hunting time selector; Defender XDR retains 30 days. Also search `SenderDisplayName` for the structural form - three uppercase characters followed by `Outage Notification` or `Service Health` - and the body phrases the lure depends on, which are the held-message claim, the 24-hour deletion deadline, and the `RESTORE MESSAGES` and `TAKE ACTION` calls to action. The third observed sender domain, `planetmaison[.]com`, is in Microsoft's indicator table but not in its query's domain list; include it.

  Leg two, the URLs and the tracking chain (EmailUrlInfo joined to EmailEvents) - Microsoft's second query, which joins message URLs back to message metadata on `NetworkMessageId`:

      let campaignUrlParts = dynamic([
          "reallifeaccounting.com",
          "u113727626.ct.sendgrid.net/ls/click",
          "url.newbooker.com/ls/click",
          "162.120.17.69:8080/t/v3."
      ]);
      EmailUrlInfo
      | where Url has_any (campaignUrlParts)
      | project NetworkMessageId, Url, UrlDomain, UrlLocation
      | join kind=inner (
          EmailEvents
          | project Timestamp, NetworkMessageId, RecipientEmailAddress,
              SenderFromAddress, SenderFromDomain, SenderDisplayName,
              Subject, DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation
      ) on NetworkMessageId
      | project Timestamp, RecipientEmailAddress, SenderFromAddress,
          SenderFromDomain, SenderDisplayName, Subject, Url, UrlDomain,
          UrlLocation, DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation, NetworkMessageId
      | order by Timestamp desc

  The join is on message identity rather than on time, which is correct here - both tables describe the same message. The durable generalisation of this leg is a mail-flow search for messages that impersonate a vendor's service-health channel and whose link chain terminates off that vendor's domains, since that is the property the campaign cannot change without losing the lure.

  Leg three, who clicked (UrlClickEvents) - Microsoft's third query, and the pivot into the half that matters:

      let campaignUrlParts = dynamic([
          "reallifeaccounting.com",
          "u113727626.ct.sendgrid.net/ls/click",
          "url.newbooker.com/ls/click",
          "162.120.17.69:8080/t/v3."
      ]);
      UrlClickEvents
      | where Url has_any (campaignUrlParts)
      | project Timestamp, AccountUpn, Url, ActionType, Workload,
          NetworkMessageId, IPAddress, IsClickedThrough, UrlChain
      | order by Timestamp desc

  `IsClickedThrough` is the field that separates a blocked click from one that reached the destination, and `UrlChain` holds the redirect path. Corroborate with proxy logs for `reallifeaccounting[.]com` and the `/themelist0.html` path, because a click record and a page load are not the same event.

  Leg four, the post-click investigation (identity and mailbox audit) - Microsoft is explicit that these behaviours were not observed in the source messages and are post-click investigation targets for suspected Microsoft 365 credential phishing. For every account that clicked through, review unusual sign-ins, new devices or user agents, MFA method changes, OAuth consent grants, inbox and forwarding rule creation, delegate permissions, and unusual access to mailbox or collaboration data. The response for any user who may have entered credentials is a password reset, revocation of active sessions and refresh tokens, and verification of MFA registrations - a password reset alone leaves a stolen session alive, which is the failure mode H502 documents in a different intrusion.

  Hardening that removes the lure rather than detecting it - phishing-resistant MFA, meaning passkeys or security keys, defeats credential capture outright; Safe Links and Safe Attachments policies in Defender for Office 365 break the redirect chain; Zero-hour auto purge retroactively removes messages delivered before the intelligence existed; and the user-facing rule is worth stating plainly in awareness material, because it is a single sentence that generalises past this campaign - legitimate Microsoft 365 service-health communications do not send you to a non-Microsoft domain to recover held mail, and service issues are verified in the Microsoft 365 admin center.

  Scope note: three existing hunts carry the spearphishing-link technique and none covers this behaviour. H303 keys on self-spoofed mail where the recipient is also the forged sender, carrying homoglyph-substituted content; H346 keys on an executable delivered as a `.pif` from a link advertising an image, which is payload delivery rather than credential capture - and Microsoft explicitly observed no payload here. The distinction this hunt adds is vendor-channel impersonation with an off-vendor destination, personalised per-recipient tracking URLs delivered through shared email-service-provider infrastructure, and a structural subject and display-name grammar designed to defeat simple pattern matching. H502 is the hunt for what an actor does with a cloud account after this kind of capture succeeds, and is the right follow-on for any account that clicked through. H217 covers post-theft use of a hijacked M365 session.

# H514

Of all the emails a Microsoft 365 user is conditioned to act on quickly, a service-health notification is close to the top. On 13 November 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence watched a coordinated, high-volume campaign impersonate exactly that.

The subjects read `Outage – ` followed by six random characters. The display names were a three-character, organization-style prefix followed by `Outage Notification` or `Service Health`. One body claimed an Exchange Online incident the previous day had held four messages, gave a 24-hour deletion deadline, and offered a `RESTORE MESSAGES` button. Three sender domains carried it, the links were generated per recipient through email-delivery and click-tracking infrastructure, and the chain ended at `reallifeaccounting[.]com`.

Microsoft is careful about what it does and does not claim. The sample set was link-based: no QR codes, no attachments beyond EML, and no evidence of any executable, archive or document payload. The assessment is that this was likely credential phishing, but credential submission and account compromise were not directly observed. Messages in tenants protected by Defender for Office were blocked or quarantined, and Microsoft saw no indicators of compromise.

Which makes this a hunt with an unusual shape. The structural detections are cheap and the expected answer is nothing. The half that matters is what you do with the users who clicked anyway — because the campaign's objective was an account, and the evidence of success does not live in mail.

## Why

- The lure impersonates the vendor's own trusted channel, and the one durable discriminator is the destination. Legitimate Microsoft service-health mail does not send a user to a non-Microsoft domain to recover held messages, which is a rule that generalises past this campaign, past this actor, and into user awareness material as a single sentence.
- The evasion is structural, so the detection has to be structural too. Randomised subject tokens, per-target display-name prefixes and recipient-specific tracking URLs exist specifically to defeat indicator matching — and counting distinct URLs will understate the footprint, because no two recipients got the same link.
- Shared delivery infrastructure makes the obvious response the wrong one. Blocking a bulk email provider's click-tracking host breaks the organisation's own mail, which is why Microsoft's guidance is to combine sender, content, URL and behavioural signals rather than block the provider.
- The mail half is where most teams stop and the identity half is where the damage is. Clicks are only a lead; sign-ins, MFA changes, OAuth grants, inbox rules and delegate permissions are where a successful credential capture becomes visible, and that review has to be driven from the click list.
- A verification hunt with an expected result of nothing is still worth running. Microsoft reports no observed compromise in protected environments — but the tenant that has not checked cannot distinguish "protected" from "unexamined", and the queries cost minutes.
