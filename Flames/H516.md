title: "The visitors' log in /api/tags: stranger-created models on a self-hosted Ollama server"
hypothesis: >-
  An unauthenticated model runtime does not need to be exploited to be used. Rapid7's November 2026
  snapshot of 20,354 internet-facing hosts found that Ollama's API lets any caller pull a model from
  any registry, copy or create models, and push them elsewhere - so on an exposed server the model
  list itself becomes a record of what strangers asked it to do. Rapid7 states that more than half
  of the exposed Ollama servers in this scan have already been touched by someone who is not the
  owner, and that an unauthenticated /api/tags call is a read-only request that on thousands of
  machines returns something close to a visitors' log. The artefacts are specific - model names
  prefixed with a non-official registry at a raw IP and port, names pulled through free tunnels or
  throwaway registries such as Cloudflare quick tunnels, ttl.sh, bore.pub and Pinggy, marker names
  in the shape of beacon, verify, probe and pwn, 712 names that embed the IP address of the server
  they sit on, names ending in -copy-test, premium aliases such as gpt-4o and claude-3-opus sitting
  next to tinyllama, and on 55 hosts Base64 or hex strings that decode to file paths. Hunt your own
  model runtimes by reading their model lists and treating any of these artefacts as evidence the
  host has been used by someone else.

false_positive_notes: |
  Researchers and red teams pull from unusual places legitimately. An internal model registry on a private IP and port looks structurally identical to an attacker's callback registry, so the discriminator is whether the address is one you approved, not whether it is a raw IP. Build the approved-registry list first; without it this hunt flags your own MLOps pipeline.

  Rapid7 is explicit that these indicators are name-based and inferential. The report states third-party activity indicators are inferred from model names only, and that a clean list doesn't prove a clean host, since names can be deleted. So a negative result is weak evidence and a positive result needs host-level corroboration before anyone rebuilds a server.

  The premium-alias pattern is labelled a hypothesis in the source, not an observation. Rapid7 writes that the trio `gpt-4o`, `gpt-4` and `claude-3-opus`, found together on 1,190 hosts, almost always next to `tinyllama`, looks like a tiny local model copied under premium names, and then says plainly that it is either a write test, or a way to make a server look like it offers frontier models to whoever finds it next, and "I wouldn't bet on which." Treat an alias hit as a prompt to inspect, not as a confirmed compromise.

  Some of the scanned population is deception infrastructure, and the same reasoning applies inward. Rapid7 removed 429 addresses as likely sensors - 148 hosts sharing an identical five-service fingerprint and 281 that answered as two to five different products on the same port - which dropped critical findings from 387 to 39. If your organisation runs honeypots, exclude them explicitly or they will dominate the output.

  Uncensored models are not themselves evidence of intrusion. Rapid7 notes that 28% of exposed hosts carry at least one "abliterated", "heretic" or "uncensored" model and that running those is legal and often has good research reasons. The finding is the combination with unauthenticated internet exposure, not the model family.

  Non-standard ports will cause misses rather than false positives. Rapid7 found 6% of findings sat on a non-standard port, so a sweep that only checks 11434 under-counts. The report's own scan covered ports 3000, 5000, 8000, 8080, 8501, 8888 and 11434.
notes: |
  Leg one, read your own model lists. This is the whole hunt in one request per host, and it is read-only. Enumerate every model runtime in the estate and pull its tag list, then triage the names against the artefact classes Rapid7 enumerates. Run this from inside, against an inventory, not by scanning the internet for your own hosts.

      curl -s http://127.0.0.1:11434/api/tags | jq -r '.models[].name'

  Triage rules, in Rapid7's order of prevalence across the 7,675 servers that survived its filtering - any third-party artefact on 54.8%, marker or "I was here" models on 40.3%, a pull from a non-official registry on 34.7%, from a raw IP and port on 23.7%, via a free tunnel or temp registry on 22.4%, premium aliases on 15.5%, and encoded file-path payloads on 0.7%. Concretely, flag a model name that:

  - begins with a registry host that is not your approved registry, especially a bare IP and port;
  - begins with a free tunnel or throwaway registry - a `trycloudflare[.]com` subdomain, `ttl[.]sh`, `bore[.]pub` or a Pinggy endpoint;
  - contains `beacon`, `verify`, `probe` or `pwn`, or matches the recurring markers Rapid7 names - `zzvitals-beacon`, `verif_sys`, `ops-verify`;
  - embeds the host's own IP address, which Rapid7 found on 712 names and reads as target inventory-keeping;
  - ends in `-copy-test`, which indicates someone checking whether the copy endpoint works;
  - carries a Base64 or hex segment. Decode it. Rapid7 found 55 hosts whose encoded segments resolved to `/etc/shadow`, `/etc/passwd`, `/proc/self/environ`, Ollama's own systemd unit and its `override.conf`, SSH `authorized_keys`, AWS config, the Oracle Cloud metadata endpoint, and on 10 hosts the Ollama private key itself.

  Leg two, the five-stage sequence, for scoping what a hit means. Rapid7 reconstructs it from the artefacts as find, list, call back, mark, reach - mass-scan port 11434 and friends with no authentication to defeat; read `/api/tags` for models, cloud routing and earlier visitors; pull from an IP or tunnel the caller controls, which confirms reachability and egress; create or copy a model named after the host as proof of write access; then craft manifests that point at local files, secrets and keys, or ride the owner's cloud account. The report notes not every host shows every stage. A marker model proves stage four, which means stages one to three already succeeded - so a marker is not a scanning artefact, it is a write.

  Leg three, the callback as egress evidence. A pull from an address the caller controls is, in Rapid7's words, a clean, reliable way to confirm that a target is reachable, writable and able to make outbound connections, and the report characterises it as classic server-side request forgery, offered as a feature. Check egress logs from model subnets for the registries Rapid7 tabulates - `24.144.109[.]68`, `103.249.201[.]108`, `76.13.49[.]213` and `62.31.247[.]107` on port 9882 with `trigger/benign` and `trigger/plamo2-<id>` path patterns, and `43.143.113[.]68` with `library/tens` and `185.165.171[.]21` with `recon/probe` on port 8899. Rapid7 cautions that several look like large-scale research scanning, that "benign" in a path is a claim, not a guarantee, and to treat these as context for hunting, not as attribution. Also sweep for short-lived tunnel endpoints, which rotate and will not match a static list.

  Leg four, the file-read and persistence targets. Where an encoded path decoded to something, check whether the file was actually touched. Rapid7 groups the decoded targets by purpose - host fingerprinting on 47 hosts, secrets in environment or service config on 28, credentials and keys on 15, persistence paths on 11, and the cloud metadata endpoint on 1 - and flags that `/etc/ld.so.preload` and new systemd service files are persistence locations, not reading material. On the mechanism, Rapid7 says this mirrors the technique Wiz published as Probllama, CVE-2024-37032, a path-traversal payload in a model manifest's digest field giving arbitrary file write through `/api/pull`, file read through `/api/push`, and code execution by corrupting `/etc/ld.so.preload`, fixed in Ollama 0.1.34 in May 2024. The report is careful that it cannot tell from the lists whether any target was still on a vulnerable build, so the attempt proves intent, not success - confirm your own build version before concluding anything.

  Leg five, the paid-account angle. Rapid7 observes that 79% of exposed Ollama hosts list at least one `:cloud` model, and that hybrid mode routes those requests through the local daemon to ollama.com when the host has been signed in with `ollama signin`, which links a local ed25519 key in `~/.ollama/id_ed25519` to the user's account. The interpretation offered is that an exposed, signed-in server can provide access to someone else's paid subscription, with the bill landing on the owner, and that this is the self-hosted version of LLMjacking requiring no exploit at all. Rapid7 labels as hypothesis that a listed cloud tag does not prove the host is signed in, and states it did not test inference - so check for the key on disk rather than inferring from the tag. On a hit, revoke the Ollama key on the linked ollama.com account and check cloud usage for spend you don't recognise.

  Dwell time, which is the argument for running this at all. Rapid7 found Unix timestamps embedded in marker and probe names ranging from 10 June to 3 November 2026, with some markers created in June still in place on 10 November, and notes this is a lower bound because only names embedding a 10-digit timestamp are counted. The report's conclusion is that when a stranger's model sits on your server for five months, the vulnerability isn't the main problem - the main problem is that nobody owns the server.

  Containment, per Rapid7's own guidance - if you find one, treat the host as compromised, not misconfigured. Rotate whatever lives in its environment and service files, revoke the Ollama key, check cloud usage, and rebuild rather than clean. Note also that only 0.3% of all findings were served over HTTPS, so credentials and prompts on these hosts were in cleartext as well.

  Scope note on existing coverage. A grep for `11434` and for `api/tags` across every hunt body returns nothing, and the existing AI-infrastructure hunts all target managed cloud inference rather than a self-hosted daemon - H446 covers enabling a managed inference service in a victim cloud project with a stolen identity, H447 covers bulk API-key validity testing with single-token requests, H448 covers short-lived cloud identities created to spread inference quota, H449 covers key regeneration abuse inside a self-hosted LiteLLM proxy, M040 covers a stolen cloud key turned toward the victim's own AI endpoints, and M064 covers an infostealer POSTing its haul to a commercial provider for appraisal. The closest of those is H449, which is also a self-hosted gateway, but it turns on an authenticated virtual key calling a privileged endpoint - this hunt has no authentication to abuse and reads a public model list instead. B076 is the baseline this hunt depends on.

# H516

Exposure data normally tells you about risk. Rapid7's November 2026 snapshot of 20,354 internet-facing hosts told them about activity, and the difference is the whole point.

Ollama's API has no authentication. It also lets any caller pull a model from any registry, copy or create models, and push them elsewhere. Put those two facts together and the model list stops being configuration and becomes a guest book — because every stranger who pulled, copied or created something left the name behind. Rapid7's phrasing is that an unauthenticated `/api/tags` call is a read-only request, and on thousands of machines it returns something close to a visitors' log.

More than half the exposed servers had been signed. Not theoretically vulnerable — visibly used. Model names that were really callback addresses at raw IPs and ports. Names pulled through Cloudflare quick tunnels, `ttl.sh`, `bore.pub` and Pinggy. Markers called `zzvitals-beacon`, `verif_sys` and `ops-verify`. 712 names with the host's own IP stamped into them, which is somebody keeping inventory. Names ending `-copy-test`, which is somebody checking the copy endpoint works. And on 55 hosts, Base64 and hex that decoded into a post-exploitation checklist: `/etc/shadow`, `authorized_keys`, AWS config, `/proc/self/environ`, the Ollama private key, `/etc/ld.so.preload`.

Some of those markers were created in June and were still sitting there in November.

The hunt is one read-only request per host, run from the inside. The hard part is knowing which hosts you have.

## Why

- The evidence is already sitting in a read-only API response. Most compromise assessment requires telemetry someone has to have been collecting. This requires one `GET` against a service you own, and it returns a list of things other people did to it — which makes it among the cheapest high-yield checks available on an AI estate.
- It separates "open" from "occupied", which is the distinction exposure scanning usually cannot make. Rapid7's argument is that "Is it open?" is the easy part and "Has it been touched?" is the useful part, and for model runtimes the answer is in the banner-adjacent data rather than requiring an intrusion investigation.
- A marker model is proof of write access, not of scanning. Creating or copying a model is a state-changing call. If a stranger's name is in your list, they got past find and list, confirmed your egress with a callback, and wrote. The read-only artefact is evidence of a write that already happened.
- These hosts are the ones least likely to be monitored. Rapid7's interpretation is that machines like these rarely appear in a CMDB — rented on a credit card by a team that needed compute quickly and connected to company data later — so the observation that your attack surface now includes your developers' expense reports is the operative one. No agent, no logs, no owner, five months of dwell.
- The daemon is becoming a credential broker, not just CPU. With 79% of exposed hosts listing a `:cloud` model and hybrid mode routing through a local ed25519 key in `~/.ollama/id_ed25519`, compromising the box is about access and money rather than cycles — and the bill lands on the owner with no exploit involved.
- Embedding models raise the stakes beyond the host. Rapid7 notes 28% also run embedding models such as `nomic-embed-text` or `bge-m3`, and reads those servers as likely wired into retrieval pipelines that read internal documents — which makes an exposed box a bridge, not an island.
