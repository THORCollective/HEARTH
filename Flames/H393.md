false_positive_notes: |
  Credentials are added to applications legitimately and often. Secrets expire and are rotated, CI/CD
  pipelines mint credentials as part of deployment, and identity governance tooling manages application
  credentials as its job. The finding is not "a secret was added".
  The narrowing that makes this operational is the actor-target pair. A developer or an administrator
  rotating a secret produces an audit record initiated by a USER. A pipeline rotating its own credential
  produces a record where the initiating application and the target application are the SAME object.
  What Huntress observed is neither: an application writing a credential into a DIFFERENT application,
  with no user anywhere in the chain. That combination is rare, and in most tenants the result set will
  be small enough to review individually.
  Two further signals help rank what comes back. The expiry date is one — Huntress notes the planted
  secrets were set to expire in 2028, which is a long life for a credential and longer than many
  organisations' rotation policies permit. The target is the other: a credential on an MSP management
  application or a security product is categorically worse than one on a line-of-business app, because
  those applications are often consented into many tenants and hold broad permissions.
  Failures matter as much as successes. In the observed tenant, "Six of the changes succeeded and three

  Expect a burst of legitimate records when a tenant onboards new tooling or migrates an application.

  A clean audit result is not proof of absence. The audit log does not record the credential's value or

# H393

The attacker's problem, two minutes after getting in, was that their access had an off switch.

A stolen vendor credential works until somebody rotates it. So the application — now holding Global Administrator, which it had granted itself four minutes earlier — ran a Python script and wrote fresh client secrets into two other applications in the tenant. One was an MSP management application. The other was Huntress's own security platform application. The new secrets were set to expire in 2028.

Nine changes in five minutes. Six succeeded, three failed, including a certificate that Microsoft rejected on a thumbprint mismatch before the client secrets went in and worked.

That is the whole mechanic, and it is worth being precise about why it is so effective. A client secret is just a way for an application to prove it is itself. Whoever creates one sees its value at creation. From that moment they can sign in as that application, from anywhere, with every permission the application already holds — and as Huntress puts it, "App-only sign-ins involve no user, no device, and no MFA prompt."

So rotating the ConnectWise credential closes the front door and leaves two the attacker cut themselves.

The targets are what turn this from persistence into a much larger problem. An MSP management application is frequently consented into every downstream customer tenant, so a secret on it is a path outward rather than a foothold inward. A security product's application holds whatever that product is permitted to read, which is usually a great deal. Huntress's advice to review management and security tooling first is not politeness about their own app being hit; it reflects where the blast radius actually is.

For hunting, the useful shape is the actor-target pair.

Secrets get added all the time — rotation, deployment pipelines, governance tooling. But a human rotating a secret leaves an audit record initiated by a *user*. A pipeline rotating its own credential leaves one where the initiating and target applications are the *same* object. Neither describes this. Here an application wrote a credential into a *different* application with no user anywhere in the chain, and that combination is rare enough in most tenants to read line by line.

Do not filter on success. Three of the nine changes failed, and the failures are evidence of the same hands.

One limitation to plan around: the audit log records that a credential was added and by whom, but not what was added. Huntress is direct about it — confirming the actual state of a tenant means enumerating credentials through Graph, not reading the log. Run both. The log tells you something happened in the window; Graph tells you what is sitting on your applications right now, including anything planted before your retention begins.

And disabling the compromised app does not undo any of this. Credentials already written stay written.

## Why

- **It converts a revocable foothold into independent access.** Rotating the vendor's credential does not touch a secret written into a different application, which is why Huntress calls rotation necessary but not enough.
- **The actor-target pair is a precise filter.** An application writing a credential into another application, with no user in the record, separates cleanly from both human rotation and self-rotation by pipelines.
- **The targets chosen maximise reach.** An MSP management application is consented across customer tenants and a security product holds broad permissions, so the grafted secrets inherit far more than the original.
- **App-only credentials bypass the controls built for people.** No user, no device, no MFA prompt, and Conditional Access policies that exclude an application leave the attacker's infrastructure unblocked there too.
- **Failed attempts carry the same signal as successful ones.** Three of nine changes failed in the observed tenant; filtering on success would have hidden a third of the activity.
- **Long expiry is a usable ranking signal.** Secrets set to expire in 2028 outlast most rotation policies, so credential lifetime sorts the review queue without needing to know the attacker.
- **The audit log cannot answer the question on its own.** It omits the credential detail entirely, so present-state enumeration through Graph is the only way to know what is live on an application today.
- **Containment intuitions are wrong here.** Disabling the abused application leaves every planted credential in place and working.
