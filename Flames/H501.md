false_positive_notes: |
  Every action in this hunt has a legitimate administrative use, which is why the hunt is built on the combination and on reconciliation rather than on any single event. Administrators do download user lists during audits and onboarding, do query the security investigation tool during real investigations, and do occasionally run tenant exports for migration or legal hold. Mandiant's own framing of the user-list export is that admins may do this legitimately and it should be heavily scrutinised, particularly when performed from unknown devices.

  Legitimate alert triage involves viewing and sometimes closing Alert Center notifications. The suspicious variant is narrower: deletion rather than closure, performed by a principal who is the subject of the alert, or targeting critical-severity alerts. Treat a batch delete that includes a government-backed attack alert as an incident regardless of who performed it.

  Tenant-wide exports should be rare enough to reconcile individually against change management. If the organisation cannot say how often Customer Takeout is legitimately used, that is the first thing the hunt produces - and in most tenants the honest answer is never.

  Discovery-tool queries will be noisy in organisations whose SOC works inside the Workspace admin tooling day to day. Separate SOC principals from general Super Admins in the register so the SOC's normal activity does not mask an operator using the same screens.

  COLD RELIC deliberately defeats location-based heuristics, so do not expect geographic anomalies to flag these sessions. Mandiant records the group using residential proxy networks selected to geolocate in close geographical proximity to the victim user, specifically to avoid impossible travel and other heuristic alerting. The useful enrichment is therefore the ASN and proxy reputation of the source address, not the distance it appears to have travelled - and Mandiant recommends alerting on sudden residential ASN shifts for privileged accounts rather than on distance.

  The claim that two or more of the four actions in a day constitutes an intrusion is a proposed triage threshold, not a measured one. Mandiant describes the four behaviours in one intrusion but does not publish timing between them. Calibrate the window against the tenant's own administrative baseline before alerting on it.
notes: |
  Admin audit log leg (Google Workspace Admin audit log) - Core filter, in four parts, each reconciled against the Super Admin register and change management.

  - Tenant-wide export: event `CUSTOMER_TAKEOUT_CREATED`. Mandiant names this event explicitly and notes the distinction that matters - individual end-user Google Takeout is a user feature, whereas Customer Takeout is an administrative tenant-wide export tool. Treat every occurrence as requiring a change ticket. Administrators can also disable Google Takeout for users at the organisational level, which is the corresponding hardening action.
  - Directory export: `User List Download` events. Triage values are `actor`, `source IP`, `ASN`, `device`, `timestamp`, and whether a case or ticket exists.
  - Alert destruction: `Batch Delete Operation Performed` events in the Admin audit log, and Alert Center view, modify and delete activity. Rank by whether the acting principal is the subject of the deleted alert and by the severity of what was deleted.
  - Defensive-visibility reconnaissance: usage of the security investigation tool and the email audit log by an administrator, particularly queries scoped to the administrator's own account or to accounts involved in a current compromise.

  Correlation that defines the hunt: group all four event classes by `actor` and by day, and rank principals by how many distinct classes they touched. A Super Admin whose day includes a security-investigation query, an Alert Center deletion and a user-list download is the top of that list, and in most tenants the list will be short enough to read.

  Drive leg (Google Workspace Drive audit log) - Core filter: per-principal download volume per hour, compared against that principal's own 30-day baseline, and external sharing events where the recipient domain is a free consumer email provider. Mandiant records COLD RELIC downloading the contents of the entire organisation's cloud storage drive by two methods at once - mass sharing files to an actor-operated email account, and downloading files directly. Both are visible: `Download` events for the direct route, and `User Sharing Permissions Change` or a visibility change to `shared_externally` for the sharing route. Strong red flags: a download spike from a non-corporate ASN or a residential proxy network; a burst of external shares all to the same external address; the same principal appearing in the Admin audit log leg above.

  Also hunt the targeting rather than only the volume. Mandiant reports that alongside mass collection, COLD RELIC performs targeted queries for credentials, personnel information, email archives in `.pst` form, and documents matching its collection requirements, plus organisational rosters, contact lists, resumes and event schedules to build pretexts for future targets. Drive search and access events for those terms, by a principal already flagged, convert a volume anomaly into an intent finding. Mandiant's corresponding hardening recommendation is cloud-storage DLP rules that alert on documents containing sensitive terms such as passwords, roster and `.pst`, or that restrict mass external sharing.

  OAuth and mail-sync leg (OAuth authorisation logs) - Core filter: new OAuth authorisations granting broad mail scopes to non-standard or unapproved clients. Mandiant's detection guidance names the pattern directly - alert on `Authorize` events in the Workspace OAuth logs granting broad scopes such as full mail access to clients like Proton or Thunderbird, and audit third-party grants continuously across both Workspace and Entra ID, flagging unexpected native desktop email clients or external mail services. Mandiant observed email inbox sync via OAuth to external email clients as part of the months-long access that followed the initial theft.

  Session and persistence context - This hunt catches the collection and the cover-up; H502 covers why the access survived remediation. Mandiant records one victim organisation detecting the intrusion and resetting passwords for the affected accounts, but not revoking existing sessions, so the actor retained access - and separately records reauthentication using unrevoked attacker-enrolled passkeys that persisted across password and session resets and bypassed device registration controls. Any response to a hit in this hunt must revoke all active browser sessions, refresh tokens and third-party OAuth grants, and remove unauthorised passkeys, backup codes and MFA devices, not only reset the password.

  Scope note: HEARTH's existing Workspace and Entra hunts cover other surfaces - H288 covers a service principal reading Teams chat tenant-wide via Microsoft Graph after a client secret is added, and B043 baselines Entra service principals holding mail and chat permissions. Neither covers an interactive Super Admin performing counter-investigation in the admin tooling, nor the Workspace-specific tenant-wide Customer Takeout and Alert Center deletion events, which were absent from the hunt corpus before this hunt was written.

# H501

Mandiant published analysis on November 18, 2026 of linked intrusions by COLD RELIC — the Russian state-sponsored group formerly tracked as COLDRIVER and UNC4057, also known as Star Blizzard, Callisto and Gossamer Bear, which the US Department of Justice has assessed operates within Russia's Federal Security Service. The report traces how a late-2025 compromise at one US organisation supplied the pretexts, staff directory and identities used to compromise a second US organisation in 2026.

What makes the first case study worth a hunt of its own is what the actor did with Super Admin privileges in the victim's Google Workspace tenant. It did not only take data. It accessed the Admin Console, queried the security investigation tool and the email audit log to check what the defenders could see, and then viewed, modified and deleted Alert Center notifications to remove the forensic record of its own entry. Alongside that it exported full employee lists and initiated a domain-wide export through Customer Takeout, an administrative tenant-wide tool rather than the per-user Takeout feature.

All of it is logged, and most of it is rare. The hunt is a reconciliation exercise against a small number of high-privilege principals, and its output doubles as the governance record most tenants do not have — how often a tenant-wide export is legitimately run, and by whom.

## Why

- Counter-investigation is a distinctive behaviour with almost no benign analogue outside the SOC. An administrator looking up what the security investigation tool holds about their own account, then deleting the alerts about it, is not performing administration. Separating SOC principals from general Super Admins in a register makes that a one-query hunt.
- Deleting a government-backed attack alert is both an anti-forensic act and an unambiguous signal. Google issues those alerts when it detects state-sponsored targeting, so a principal removing one is destroying the single clearest warning the tenant received.
- Customer Takeout is a one-action, whole-tenant exfiltration path, and it is logged under a specific event name. Treating `CUSTOMER_TAKEOUT_CREATED` as requiring a ticket costs nothing in a tenant that never uses it, and catches the highest-impact single action available to a compromised Super Admin.
- Location-based detection will not help here, and relying on it is the trap. Mandiant is explicit that COLD RELIC selects residential proxies geolocating near the victim precisely to avoid impossible-travel and similar heuristics, so the enrichment that works is ASN and proxy reputation — and a sudden residential ASN shift for a privileged account, which Mandiant recommends alerting on directly.
