false_positive_notes: |
  Short-lived cloud identities are a legitimate pattern, and this hunt has to be tuned against the organisation's own automation before it is usable:
  - Ephemeral CI/CD and test identities. Integration-test suites and per-pull-request environments create and destroy service accounts constantly. These come from a known CI principal, follow a naming convention, are created in test projects, and are matched by a pipeline run. Allowlist the creating principal plus the project, not the pattern.
  - Terraform and other IaC apply/destroy cycles. A `destroy` after an `apply` produces exactly this lifecycle. The separator is the state file and the plan: the identity appears in committed configuration. An identity that never appears in any repository is the finding.
  - Workload Identity Federation and short-lived token issuance are the *correct* alternative to this pattern and do not create accounts at all - they issue tokens. If an environment has migrated to federation, any account-creation activity is itself anomalous, which makes this hunt much sharper there.
  - Provider-managed service agents are created and removed by the platform, attributed to a Google- or AWS-owned principal rather than a customer identity.
  Billing API reads are also normal for finance tooling, FinOps platforms and cost dashboards. Those use a dedicated identity with a stable schedule and no account-creation rights. The finding is the *combination*: one identity that both mints disposable children and reads cost data.
notes: |
  Google Cloud - Core filter: join `google.iam.admin.v1.CreateServiceAccount` to `google.iam.admin.v1.DeleteServiceAccount` on the service-account email, and keep pairs whose lifetime is under 24 hours. Triage values: `protoPayload.authenticationInfo.principalEmail` (the creator), `protoPayload.requestMetadata.callerIp`, `callerSuppliedUserAgent`, the child account's email and display name, `resource.labels.project_id`. Correlation: for each short-lived child, confirm a `CreateServiceAccountKey` and at least one data-plane call under that child before the delete. Then group by creator and keep creators with 3 or more such children in a rolling 24 hours. Strong red flags: display names that borrow the language of cost control - GTIG reports an actor that "created deceptive backdoor service accounts disguised as internal billing optimization utilities" - alongside `SetIamPolicy` grants of `roles/aiplatform.user` or `roles/owner` to the child.
  AWS - Core filter: the same lifecycle over `iam:CreateUser` -> `iam:CreateAccessKey` -> first use of that `accessKeyId` -> `iam:DeleteUser`, within 24 hours. Triage values: `userIdentity.arn` (the creator), `userIdentity.accessKeyId`, `sourceIPAddress`, `userAgent`, `requestParameters.userName`, `requestParameters.policyArn`. Correlation: `sts:GetCallerIdentity` immediately after key creation is the near-universal first act of an operator confirming a fresh credential works, and it is cheap to pivot on. Strong red flags: `iam:AttachUserPolicy` attaching an administrative policy to a brand-new user; the created user's only data-plane activity being `bedrock:InvokeModel`; deletion occurring minutes after the last invocation.
  Azure / Entra ID - Core filter: Entra audit `Add service principal` followed by `Add service principal credentials` and `Delete service principal` on the same object ID inside 24 hours, with an Azure role assignment in between. Triage values: `initiatedBy`, `targetResources`, `ipAddress`, `activityDisplayName`. Correlation: service principal sign-ins for the short-lived object, and `Microsoft.CognitiveServices` or `Microsoft.Compute` writes under it.
  The billing-read tell - Core filter: for every identity flagged as a creator, query cost-management and billing API access in the same window. GTIG describes the actor "auditing the cloud provider’s billing interface to monitor financial impact" as part of the rotation strategy. Triage values: the API called, the scope requested (whole billing account versus one project), and whether this identity has ever read cost data before. An operations identity that mints service accounts has no business reason to read a billing account's spend, and the first-ever cost read by such an identity is a strong confirmation. Note that billing data access is a separate log category in each cloud and is frequently not collected - check that it is enabled before concluding the signal is absent.
  Why the lifecycle, not the creation, is the signal - Core filter: creation events alone are too common to work with in any environment that has automation. The discriminating measurement is the *distribution of identity lifetimes* per creating principal. Keep a 30-day baseline of (creator, median child lifetime, child count per day); a creator whose median child lifetime drops into the single-digit hours while its child count rises is the shape to chase. Strong red flags: children created and destroyed in a region or project the creator has no other footprint in; children whose creation times are evenly spaced, indicating a loop rather than a human.
  Linking to the objective - Core filter: once a rotation cluster is confirmed, total the inference, compute and GPU-provisioning activity across every child. GTIG reports this strategy adopted specifically "To bypass rate limits", alongside Pay-As-You-Go subscription of victim accounts and premium model selection, and documents one actor executing "more than 500,000 LLM API calls in less than eight hours". The per-child volume may each sit inside a normal band while the sum does not - which is precisely why a per-identity threshold misses it and the lifecycle grouping does not.
  Expected, not observed by GTIG: the same burn-and-rotate shape is a general answer to any per-identity quota, so it should appear around compute quotas, storage request limits and send-rate limits on messaging services, not only model inference. GTIG reports it in the context of LLM rate limits.

# H448

Per-identity rate limits are the main thing standing between a stolen cloud credential and unbounded model spend. Google Threat Intelligence Group reports an actor that solved the problem the obvious way: "Another threat actor subscribed victim accounts to Pay-As-You-Go agreements, selecting premium foundation models and launching high-volume inference streams." To get past the quotas, GTIG writes, the actor deployed a burn-and-rotate strategy - in its own words, "continuously creating and deleting short-lived service accounts while auditing the cloud provider’s billing interface to monitor financial impact."

Each half of that is a detection opportunity that the other makes legible. Creating service accounts is unremarkable in any environment with automation. Deleting them is unremarkable too. What is not unremarkable is a single identity producing a stream of children whose entire existence - creation, key issuance, use, deletion - fits inside a few hours, several times over, in a project where nothing else behaves that way. And an identity that mints disposable accounts while also reading the billing account's spend is not doing FinOps; it is checking how much runway is left on someone else's card.

The rotation also defeats the per-identity controls an organisation is most likely to have. Quota alarms, anomaly detections and spend caps that are scoped to a principal all reset with each new child, so ten identities each running inside a normal band can sum to a bill nobody's threshold ever sees. The grouping that recovers the total is the creator, not the caller.

GTIG also notes the camouflage in the naming: an actor "created deceptive backdoor service accounts disguised as internal billing optimization utilities". An account called something like a cost-savings tool, created by automation, reading billing data, is a plausible-looking object in a cloud console - which is why this hunt is built on lifetime distribution rather than on names.

## Why

- The rotation is forced by the defence, so it persists as long as per-identity quotas do. An operator who wants throughput beyond one identity's limit has no alternative to multiple identities, and creating and destroying them is the only way to keep the standing footprint small.
- Grouping by creator restores the measurement the operator broke. Summed across a rotation cluster, the inference volume GTIG reports - over 500,000 API calls in under eight hours in one case - is plainly anomalous; per child it may not be. The creator edge in the IAM graph is what makes the sum computable.
- The billing-read is a behavioural tell with almost no benign counterpart in the same identity. Automation that creates service accounts does not read cost data, and cost tooling does not create service accounts. The intersection is small enough to review by hand.
- Every event in the chain is a control-plane write and therefore logged by default in Google Cloud Admin Activity, AWS CloudTrail management events and the Entra audit log - no data-plane logging, no payload capture, and no additional cost to turn on.
