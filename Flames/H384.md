title: One RMM agent installing another - an RMM service spawning PowerShell that deploys a second remote-access product
hypothesis: >-
  The remote-management agent that got the operator in is used to install the one they intend to keep.
  Microsoft Defender Experts observed phishing campaigns in July 2026 distributing a legitimate,
  digitally signed MSP360 RMM installer (v2.5.0.67) under deceptive filenames through meeting-invite and
  tax-document lures. Following successful UAC elevation the installer registered Windows services and
  completed, and then - the behaviour this hunt targets - "the newly installed RMM.Agent.exe service
  launched PowerShell. The PowerShell process modified the execution policy for the current session and
  executed Invoke-WebRequest" to retrieve and install a ConnectWise ScreenConnect client, "creating a
  secondary remote-access channel that provided redundant access to compromised systems." The first
  product is the delivery vehicle; the second is the persistence. That chaining is the detectable event,
  and it is specific in a way neither product is on its own: a service belonging to one remote-management
  product spawning a script interpreter that downloads and installs a different remote-management
  product. No managed estate deploys RMM that way - a legitimate rollout comes from a deployment platform
  under a management identity, not from a competitor agent service.

false_positive_notes: |
  Hosts carrying more than one remote-management product are completely ordinary and are not what this
  hunt is about - managed service providers, acquisitions, vendor migrations, co-managed estates and
  help-desk escalation paths routinely leave two or three products installed side by side for years. The
  hunt is narrower: one product service spawning a script interpreter that installs a DIFFERENT product.
  That has a small number of genuine forms and they are all nameable. An MSP legitimately uses its RMM to
  deploy tooling, including other remote-access software, and that is the single largest benign
  population - so in an MSP-managed or co-managed estate this query must be scoped by whether the
  installing agent belongs to the sanctioned tenant and whether the destination it downloaded from is an
  approved one, which is exactly what the sanctioned-instance baselines exist to answer. Vendor migrations
  produce the same chain honestly, usually once, in a window that change management knows about. Software
  deployment through an RMM is the product core purpose, so a scripted install launched by an RMM agent is
  normal in isolation - the discriminators are what was installed, from where, and under whose identity.
  Two further cautions. The execution-policy change (`Set-ExecutionPolicy` scoped to the process) is
  extremely common in legitimate automation and carries almost no weight on its own; it belongs in the
  triage view, not in the filter. And `Invoke-WebRequest` from an RMM-spawned PowerShell is likewise
  routine, so the signal is the destination and the payload, not the cmdlet. Where this hunt earns its
  keep is the case Microsoft describes - the installing agent itself arrived by phishing, so neither
  product is sanctioned and neither tenant is yours.

  Sample KQL over Defender XDR, adapted from Microsoft published query - the vendor-aware parent leg.

# H384

The RMM that got them in is not the RMM they keep. It is the one that installs it.

Microsoft Defender Experts watched this run in July 2026. Phishing emails with tax-document and meeting-invite lures led to a legitimate, digitally signed MSP360 RMM installer — version 2.5.0.67, renamed to look like a business document, hosted across Amazon S3, Cloudflare R2, Dropbox, GitLab, Supabase and attacker infrastructure in rotation. The user ran it from their Downloads folder, clicked through UAC, and MSP360 installed itself properly: two Windows services, autorun entries for its tray components, and an inbound firewall rule allowing UDP 48678 to `C:\Program Files\RMM Agent\RMM.Agent.exe`.

Then `RMM.Agent.exe` — now a running service, belonging to a real signed product — launched PowerShell, changed the session execution policy, and used `Invoke-WebRequest` to download and install a ConnectWise ScreenConnect client pointed at the operator's own infrastructure.

Microsoft's phrase for the result is "redundant access." The more useful way to read it is as an attack on the dependency graph.

Think about what a responder finds a week later. There is a rogue ScreenConnect client beaconing out. Where did it come from? It was installed locally, over HTTP, by a PowerShell process whose parent was a *signed service belonging to a legitimately installed backup and management product*. And MSP360 itself looks like software somebody meant to have — real vendor, real signature, real services, real firewall rule, sitting in `Program Files`. The phishing email is two steps removed from anything that looks wrong.

HEARTH already covers most of this campaign from other directions. [[H279]] covers a signed RMM agent installed by `msiexec` from a user path. [[H306]] covers an operator using ScreenConnect's own `RunFile` capability to run code on connected machines — which is exactly how the follow-on tooling arrived here, staged under the user's Documents and OneDrive Documents directories with filenames imitating Windows utilities. [[H341]] covers RMM-as-service persistence. [[H374]] covers two instances of the *same* product where the second uninstalls the first.

What none of them covers is one product's service installing a *different* product. That is the uncovered link, and it is a narrow one: no managed estate deploys remote-access software that way. A real rollout comes from a deployment platform under a management identity, not from a competitor's agent service.

The honest caveat is MSPs, and it is a big one. An MSP legitimately uses its RMM to deploy tooling, sometimes including other remote-access software, and in a co-managed estate this query needs scoping by whether the installing agent belongs to the sanctioned tenant and whether the download destination is approved. Two things in the filter carry no weight at all and belong only in the triage view: `Set-ExecutionPolicy` scoped to the process is ubiquitous in legitimate automation, and `Invoke-WebRequest` from an RMM-spawned shell is the product doing its job. The signal is *what* was installed, from *where*, and under *whose* identity.

Two smaller things are worth taking from this report. The installer recorded its own progress — "Begin installation", "End installation" — using `eventcreate.exe`, which is a legitimate Windows binary for writing arbitrary event log entries and which has no HEARTH coverage whatsoever. It is a nearly free query with a tiny benign population, and it means the attacker helpfully wrote lines into the Application log. And Microsoft notes the installer sometimes *failed* to elevate, leaving `System.dll`, `nsExec.dll` and `UAC.dll` in a user-writable path with no completed install. That failure case is the cleaner signal, because there is no legitimate product on the host to explain the debris.

## Why

- **Chaining severs the surviving channel from the delivery.** The rogue ScreenConnect was installed locally by a signed service, so tracing it backwards leads to a plausible commercial product rather than to a phishing email.
- **The first product provides legitimacy, not just access.** Real vendor, real signature, real services, real firewall rule, installed in `Program Files`. It survives a glance in a way a dropper in `%TEMP%` does not.
- **Cross-product installation has no legitimate form outside MSP estates.** A sanctioned rollout comes from a deployment platform under a management identity. One vendor's agent service installing a competitor's client does not happen by accident.
- **Neither the hash nor the hosting domain is durable.** Microsoft notes the actor rotated lures, filenames and hosting — across five legitimate cloud services — while shipping the same installer. The behaviour is the only stable thing.
- **The execution-policy change and the download cmdlet are not the signal.** Both are routine for RMM automation. Treating them as filter criteria is how this hunt turns into noise; they belong in the triage view.
- **`eventcreate.exe` is an unhunted LOLBin with a tiny benign population.** An installer narrating itself into the Application log is unusual, cheap to query, and leaves attacker-written log entries to read.
- **The failed-elevation case is the cleaner detection.** Installer components left in a user-writable path with no completed install have no legitimate product on the host to explain them.
- **ScreenConnect `RunFile` staging paths are a direct file query.** Tooling arriving under `Documents\ScreenConnect\Temp\` and the OneDrive equivalent, named to imitate Windows utilities, is specific and immediately checkable.
- **The firewall rule is a durable, checkable artefact.** An inbound allow for UDP 48678 bound to an agent binary path persists long after the installer is gone.
