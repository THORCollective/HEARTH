false_positive_notes: |
  Self-addressed mail has a substantial and genuinely important legitimate population, and this is the axis that decides whether the hunt is usable. The common sources: users emailing themselves notes, links and attachments as a scratchpad, which is extremely common and entirely benign; calendar and meeting notifications where the organiser is the recipient; scanners, multifunction printers and "scan to email" appliances that send as the scanning user; ticketing, HR, expense, DLP, backup and monitoring systems configured to send on a user's behalf; mailing lists and distribution groups that reflect a member's own post back to them; and mobile or desktop clients using "send to self" sync features. Every one of those, however, differs on the same structural field: they originate INTERNALLY or from an authenticated, allowlisted relay, and they pass or are exempted from DMARC. The malicious population arrives from the public internet with a forged header From. So the filter that matters is not "From equals To" — it is "From equals To AND the message entered from an external connector AND DMARC did not pass." Tune by enumerating the legitimate internal relays and applications first and excluding them by connector or source IP rather than by sender address, because the sender address is precisely what the attacker controls. Remaining false positives cluster around misconfigured third-party senders (marketing platforms, survey tools, e-signature services) that send as a tenant user without a DKIM record — these are real findings of a different kind and are worth fixing rather than suppressing, because each one is an inbound path an attacker can imitate. The homoglyph condition should be treated as an enrichment that raises confidence, never as a required term: it is a property of one kit, while the self-spoof geometry is a property of the technique.
notes: |
  The core detection, self-spoof geometry - Core filter: inbound internet mail where the header From domain is a domain your tenant owns and the From address equals the To address, and DMARC did not pass. Defender XDR KQL starting point - `EmailEvents | where EmailDirection == "Inbound" | where tolower(SenderFromAddress) == tolower(RecipientEmailAddress) | extend DMARC = tostring(parse_json(AuthenticationDetails).DMARC) | where DMARC !in~ ("pass") | project Timestamp, NetworkMessageId, SenderFromAddress, SenderMailFromAddress, RecipientEmailAddress, Subject, SenderIPv4, DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation, DMARC, AuthenticationDetails`. Triage values - `SenderIPv4` and its ASN, `SenderMailFromAddress` (the envelope sender, which in a self-spoof usually does NOT match the header From and is the fastest tell), `DeliveryAction` and `DeliveryLocation`. Strong red flags - `DeliveryLocation` is `Inbox` rather than `Quarantine` or `JunkFolder` despite the DMARC result, and the envelope MAIL FROM is an unrelated domain or is empty.
  Why the envelope-versus-header split is the reliable field - A self-spoof requires forging the header `From:` because that is what the user sees. The envelope sender is usually left as something the sending infrastructure can actually deliver bounces to, so `SenderFromAddress` and `SenderMailFromAddress` diverge. Legitimate self-addressed mail sent through your own tenant has them agree. Querying that divergence alone, without any subject-line logic, is a durable detection that survives kit changes.
  Subject-line homoglyph substitution - Core filter: subjects containing a lowercase `l` where a capital `I` belongs. The Knight Office campaign used `lmportant`, `Slgnature` and `VERlVIED` to evade keyword matching. Practical approach - normalise the subject by lowercasing and then testing against the intended keyword set, which collapses `lmportant` and `Important` to the same token and makes the evasion self-defeating; then alert on the RAW subject differing from the normalised match. Observed lure patterns from the campaign - `Reminder: Signature Required - Approval Pending Your Review!!! Ref ID-<RANDOM>`, `You Missed (2) lmportant VoiceMessage`, and `VERlVIED: Caller left [digits] minutes`. Broaden beyond the Latin-lookalike case to full Unicode confusables (Cyrillic а, е, о, р, с and Greek ο, ν) by flagging subjects that mix scripts within a single word — a message whose subject contains both Latin and Cyrillic codepoints inside one token is essentially never legitimate business mail.
  Connecting the lure to the landing page - Core filter: `EmailUrlInfo` joined to `UrlClickEvents` on `NetworkMessageId`, to identify which recipients actually clicked. The campaign chained redirects through legitimate tracking parameters (Monday.com was observed as an intermediate hop) before reaching the AiTM proxy, so the first URL in the message is frequently a clean, reputable domain and reputation-based URL filtering does not help. Strong red flags in the final landing domain - newly registered, unusual TLD (the campaign used twenty-five `.vu` domains), self-signed TLS certificate, and a page title such as `Login — KNIGHT OFFICE`. Pivot immediately from any confirmed click to H302 to establish whether a session was actually captured and replayed.
  Infrastructure characteristics worth recording - The campaign sent lures from `154.127.53[.]78` and operated its console at `104.37.188[.]94` / `idoej[.]com`, with the phishing pages behind Cloudflare Turnstile (sitekey `0x4AAAAAADrkE-VuOnNDfr6W`) to keep automated crawlers and security scanners away from the credential form. A Turnstile or CAPTCHA challenge in front of a Microsoft-branded login page is itself anomalous — Microsoft does not gate its own sign-in that way — and is a useful enrichment when investigating a reported lure.
  Standing configuration to verify while hunting - Check whether any inbound connector, transport rule or IP allowlist permits external mail to bypass spoof intelligence or authentication checks. Self-spoofed mail should be stopped by DMARC enforcement at `p=reject` plus anti-spoofing protection, so a delivered self-spoof frequently indicates a policy gap rather than a novel evasion. Enumerating those bypass paths is a durable hardening outcome from this hunt regardless of whether a live campaign is found.

# H303

The Knight Office phishing kit Huntress analysed in 2026 delivered its lures with a header trick that is old, cheap, and still remarkably effective: the messages appeared to come from the recipient themselves. From, To and Return-Path all named the target's own address. A message from yourself inherits every trust cue a mail client attaches to internal senders — it is commonly rendered with your own display name and photo, it frequently escapes the "external sender" banner that organisations rely on, and it sidesteps the single piece of user training that actually sticks, which is to check whether the sender is someone you recognise.

The same messages carried a second evasion aimed at the filter rather than the user. Subjects were written with deliberate character substitutions — `lmportant` with a lowercase L, `Slgnature`, `VERlVIED` — so that keyword rules looking for `Important` or `Signature` matched nothing while the rendered text remained perfectly readable to a human in most sans-serif fonts. Combined, the two evasions target the two halves of the defence separately: the geometry defeats the human check, the homoglyphs defeat the machine check.

What makes this huntable rather than merely annoying is that the geometry is a structural property, not an indicator. To make the message appear to come from the user, the attacker must forge the header `From:` on a message that entered the tenant from the public internet, and that is a queryable condition that no amount of infrastructure rotation changes. The large legitimate population of self-addressed mail — people emailing themselves notes, scan-to-email appliances, calendar notifications, ticketing systems — differs on exactly the right field: it originates internally or from an authenticated relay and it passes DMARC. So the hunt is a three-term filter over telemetry every Microsoft 365 tenant already has, and a delivered result frequently exposes a policy gap (an over-permissive inbound connector, DMARC not at enforcement) that is worth fixing whether or not this particular campaign is present. H302 covers what happens after a recipient clicks, and M042 models the proxy geometry that follows a successful session capture.

## Why

- The self-spoof defeats the specific control most organisations lean on hardest. External-sender banners and "do you know this person" training both key on the apparent sender, and both are neutralised by a message that appears to originate from the recipient's own account.
- The evasion is structural and therefore durable to hunt. Domains, IPs and TLS certificates rotate freely, but making a message look like it came from the user requires forging the header From on inbound internet mail — a condition that is directly queryable and that the technique cannot discard without abandoning its own advantage.
- The envelope-versus-header divergence gives a high-precision field that needs no keyword logic. A genuine self-addressed message sent through the tenant has matching header From and envelope MAIL FROM; a forgery normally does not, because the attacker still needs bounces to go somewhere deliverable.
- The homoglyph substitution is self-defeating once it is normalised. Lowercasing the subject collapses `lmportant` onto `important`, so the evasion becomes the detection — and alerting on the difference between raw and normalised subject generalises well beyond this one kit to full Unicode confusable abuse.
- Reputation-based URL defences do not help here, because the campaign chained its redirects through legitimate tracking services before reaching the phishing page. The first link in the message is a reputable domain, which pushes the detection value back onto the message geometry.
- A delivered self-spoof is usually evidence of a configuration gap as well as an attack. Spoof intelligence plus DMARC at enforcement should stop this, so the hunt reliably produces a hardening outcome — an enumerated list of inbound paths permitted to bypass authentication — independent of whether a live campaign is found.
