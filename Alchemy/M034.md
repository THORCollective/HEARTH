false_positive_notes: |
  Bulk file access in M365 is normal, high-volume, and business-critical. The naive volumetric rule is
  unworkable. Expect substantial legitimate activity from:
  - Migration and consolidation projects — tenant-to-tenant moves, file-share-to-SharePoint migrations, ShareGate
    and Migration Manager. These produce the largest and broadest access patterns in the entire tenant and will
    dominate any unmodelled ranking.
  - Backup and archiving connectors — Veeam, AvePoint, Druva, Barracuda — which by design enumerate and read
    everything, continuously, forever.
  - eDiscovery, legal hold, and compliance collection.
  - The OneDrive sync client performing an initial full sync after a device rebuild, a new starter's onboarding,
    or a re-provisioned laptop. This is the single most common benign "sudden bulk download" and it is
    indistinguishable from exfiltration on volume alone.
  - DLP, CASB, and content-classification scanners.
  - Executive assistants, project managers, and researchers who legitimately have and use broad access.
  - Departing employees taking their own work — a real problem, but an HR and insider-risk problem with a
    different response path than a ransomware operator.
  This is why the hunt is scored rather than thresholded. The model must:
  (1) Baseline PER PRINCIPAL, not globally. A backup connector with a stable, enormous, unchanging profile scores
      near zero forever, because breadth is normal FOR IT. The signal is a principal whose own history is narrow
      suddenly going wide.
  (2) Treat named service principals, migration accounts, and connector identities as enumerated populations with
      their own baselines — not as global suppressions. A suppression on a migration account is exactly what an
      operator wants to inherit; a baseline on it still catches the account doing something new.
  (3) Weight NOVELTY heavily. Sites and libraries the principal has never touched contribute far more than
      volume within familiar ones.
  (4) Require the session context to corroborate. Bulk access is ambiguous; bulk access from a session that
      authenticated by legacy protocol, from a new ASN, without MFA, or under a newly consented application is
      not.
  (5) Score the OneDrive sync client explicitly as its own class. It has a recognisable user agent and a
      recognisable access pattern (one drive, the user's own, sequential, sustained). Model it rather than
      excluding it — an operator running under a stolen session can claim the same user agent.
  Expect the first two to three weeks of scoring to be dominated by unmodelled migration and backup identities.
  Enumerate and name them before treating any score as actionable, and re-baseline after any migration project.

  CATEGORY: Alchemy. Flames rejected — there is no discrete artifact. Every event in this hunt is a legitimate,
  permitted file operation by a validly authenticated principal; the only Flames-shaped version is a volume
  threshold, and volume is the one variable entirely under the operator's control (see below). Embers rejected,
  though it was the closer call: a baseline of "which sites does each principal normally touch" is genuinely
  required, but it is not the deliverable — the deliverable is a ranked queue, because the answer is not binary.
  A principal legitimately touches new sites every week as projects change, so first-time access alone is far
  too common to alert on, and it only becomes meaningful in JOINT extremity with rate, verb mix and session
  provenance. That joint-rarity requirement across continuous features is what makes this a model rather than a
  lookup, which is the distinction between this hunt and [[B037]].

  WHY THIS NEEDS A MODEL AND NOT A RULE. The obvious detection is "alert when a user downloads more than N files
  in M minutes," and Microsoft, Splunk and Elastic all ship a version of it. Deploy those — they are cheap and
  they catch the unsophisticated case. They also fail on both sides in a real tenant:
  - TOO NOISY: a new starter's first OneDrive sync, a migration engineer, and a backup connector all exceed any
    threshold low enough to catch a careful operator.
  - TOO PERMISSIVE: an operator who paces collection under the threshold, or spreads it across several
    compromised identities, never trips it. Volume is the one variable entirely under the attacker's control.
  And critically, VOLUME IS NOT EVEN THE RIGHT SIGNAL: Varonis demonstrated that SharePoint access via the
  "open in app" path and via sync are logged as `FileAccessed` and `FileSyncDownloadedFull`, NOT as
  `FileDownloaded`. A detection keyed on `FileDownloaded` misses those entirely. Any model here must consume the
  whole verb set.

  THE MODEL:
  - UNIT OF SCORING: the (principal, time-window) pair, where principal is a user OR a service principal OR an
    OAuth application identity. Score all three populations — the app-identity path is how modern M365
    collection increasingly runs, and it is invisible to user-centric detections.
  - WINDOW: rolling 60 minutes for burst features; 90 days of history for the baseline.
  - FEATURE 1 — SITE/LIBRARY BREADTH (high weight): count of DISTINCT SiteUrl and distinct document libraries
    touched in the window. Most people live in a handful of sites. Collection sweeps.
  - FEATURE 2 — NOVELTY (highest weight): the fraction of those sites the principal has NEVER accessed in the
    90-day history. A principal reading fifty familiar files is working; a principal reading fifty files across
    twelve sites it has never opened is enumerating. Novelty is the feature that survives an operator pacing
    their volume, because they still do not know which sites are familiar.
  - FEATURE 3 — VERB MIX: the distribution across FileDownloaded, FileAccessed, FileSyncDownloadedFull,
    FileSyncDownloadedPartial, FilePreviewed, and the enumeration verbs. A principal whose access is
    overwhelmingly FileAccessed or sync-verb with near-zero interactive UI verbs is being read by code, not by a
    person. This feature also closes the Varonis evasion path directly.
  - FEATURE 4 — RATE AND REGULARITY: files per minute, and the variance of inter-request intervals. Human
    browsing is irregular and bursty; a collector is regular. Low interval variance at sustained rate is
    machine-driven regardless of the claimed user agent.
  - FEATURE 5 — SESSION PROVENANCE: the sign-in backing the activity — new ASN or geography for this principal,
    hosting-provider ASN, absent or unusual MFA, legacy authentication, non-compliant or unregistered device,
    conditional-access bypass, or a token whose session ID does not correspond to any recent interactive
    sign-in. That last case is the session-hijacking signature from the advisory and should carry near-decisive
    weight.
  - FEATURE 6 — APP AND CONSENT NOVELTY: the activity running under an application registered or consented in
    the last 30 days, or under a service principal whose permission set recently expanded.
  - FEATURE 7 — SENSITIVITY CONCENTRATION: the proportion of accessed content carrying restricted sensitivity
    labels, or residing in sites flagged as crown-jewel. This is what turns a ranked queue into a prioritised
    one — it does not detect, it triages.
  - FEATURE 8 — DOWNSTREAM COUPLING: archive creation, egress to consumer file-sharing services (Mega,
    MediaFire, Dropbox personal, WeTransfer) from the same identity or its associated endpoints, or a large
    outbound transfer at the network layer within hours. Gunra's chain was OneDrive/SharePoint collection ->
    compressed archives -> Mega, so this feature closes the loop from collection to exfiltration.
  - SCORING: score each feature against the (principal) historical distribution — empirical percentile or
    z-score — and combine multiplicatively so JOINT extremity is required. A principal high on breadth alone is
    a migration engineer; a principal high on breadth AND novelty AND rate AND session provenance is the
    operator. Never let volume alone reach the top of the queue.
  - OUTPUT: a ranked daily review queue of (principal, window) tuples with contributing feature values shown, so
    an analyst sees WHY something ranked. This is a queue to work, not an alert to fire.

  VALIDATION: replay against a known-clean 90-day window and confirm the top of the queue is enumerable, nameable
  identities — migration accounts, backup connectors, onboarding syncs. If it is not, those populations are not
  yet modelled and the baseline work is incomplete. Then simulate: have an authorised tester enumerate and read
  across sites they do not normally touch, from a fresh session, and confirm they rank above the benign
  population. If they do not, the novelty and session-provenance weights are too low.

  ALSO DEPLOY THE STATIC RULES. Elastic's "M365 OneDrive/SharePoint Excessive File Downloads" and Splunk's "O365
  Exfiltration via File Access" are cheap and catch the loud case. This model is the layer that survives the
  quiet one, not a replacement for them.

# M034

Gunra pulled data out of OneDrive and SharePoint before encrypting anything — for one victim, tens of terabytes. The FBI attributes it to a purpose-built binary, `main.exe`.

The instinctive detection is a volume threshold, and every major vendor ships one. Deploy them; they are cheap and they catch the careless. But volume is the one variable entirely under the operator's control, and in a real tenant the threshold that catches a patient collector also catches a new starter's first OneDrive sync, a migration engineer, and every backup connector you own — continuously.

Worse, volume is measured against the wrong thing. Varonis showed that SharePoint access through "open in app" and through sync is recorded as `FileAccessed` and `FileSyncDownloadedFull`, not `FileDownloaded`. A detection keyed on downloads does not see it at all.

What the operator cannot control is that they do not know your tenant. They reach into sites this identity has never opened, at a machine-regular rate, through a session that authenticated in a way this person never authenticates. The migration engineer has done this every day for a year; this principal has never done it once.

## Why

- **Novelty is the feature an operator cannot economise on, and volume is the one they can.** Pacing collection below a threshold is trivial and spreading it across several compromised identities is only slightly harder. Neither helps with the fact that the identity is reaching into document libraries it has no history with — because knowing which sites are *familiar* to a given user requires the reconnaissance the operator is currently performing. Scoring the fraction of accessed sites that are novel to this principal targets the objective rather than its implementation, and it degrades gracefully as the operator gets quieter.

- **Per-principal baselining is the only unit that makes sense, and a global threshold is actively harmful.** M365 tenants are radically heterogeneous by design: a backup connector reading everything, forever, is correct behaviour; a finance analyst reading twelve unfamiliar sites in an hour is not. A single number is simultaneously too noisy for the first and too permissive for the second. Modelling per identity makes "normal for a migration account" and "normal for this analyst" two different statements — which is the only formulation that is operable in a tenant of any size.

- **The verb mix closes a published evasion path for free.** The Varonis research is not theoretical — it describes how to move data out of SharePoint without generating a single `FileDownloaded` event. Any model that consumes the full verb set, and treats a principal whose activity is overwhelmingly `FileAccessed` and sync-verb with no interactive UI events as machine-driven, is immune to that specific bypass. This costs nothing beyond querying more columns from data you already retain, and it is a strictly better foundation than the download-centric rules everyone starts with.

- **Session provenance is where this advisory's chain becomes decisive.** Gunra did not simply steal a password. They manipulated an SSL-VPN appliance's traffic handling to harvest credentials and session information from a VDI authentication portal, then hijacked those sessions. The file activity that follows looks entirely normal — right user, right permissions — but the *sign-in* backing it does not: a session ID with no corresponding interactive authentication, a hosting-provider ASN, an absent MFA claim. Joining Entra sign-in context to file activity turns an ambiguous access pattern into an explainable one, and it is the feature that most directly reflects how this actor actually operated.

- **This telemetry survives the encryption, which is why it is worth more than the endpoint signal.** In a double-extortion intrusion, endpoint telemetry is about to become unavailable — the hosts are being encrypted and the operator has been deleting logs and clearing command history along the way. The Unified Audit Log lives in Microsoft's tenant, outside the blast radius, and it records the collection phase that happens *before* the ransomware runs. Detection here is detection in the window where the outcome can still be changed; detection on the endpoint often is not.

- **The app-identity variant is invisible to user-centric detection, and it is where this is heading.** Increasingly this collection runs under a consented OAuth application or a service principal rather than a user session, and most tenants' bulk-access detections are written exclusively against user activity. Scoring service principals and application identities in the same model — with consent and permission-expansion recency as features — covers the path that a user-focused rule structurally cannot see, at no extra modelling cost.

- **Sensitivity concentration is what makes the queue workable rather than merely correct.** A ranked list of anomalous principals is only useful if an analyst can decide what to open first. Weighting by the proportion of accessed content carrying restricted labels or living in crown-jewel sites does not improve detection, but it converts a daily review queue into a triage order — which is the difference between a model that gets worked and a model that gets ignored.

- **HEARTH had no T1530 coverage at all,** despite cloud-storage collection now being a routine pre-encryption step for ransomware crews and the primary data source for double-extortion leverage.
