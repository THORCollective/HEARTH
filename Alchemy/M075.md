false_positive_notes: |
  This hunt will light up every legitimate automation in the estate on its first run, and that is the intended first outcome. Backup and archiving agents, eDiscovery and legal-hold exports, SharePoint migration jobs, data-loss-prevention scanners, records-retention sweeps and analytics connectors all read at machine rate with SDK client strings. The output of the first pass is an allowlist of sanctioned automation keyed by principal and client string; the hunt only becomes operational once that list exists.

  Client strings are self-reported and trivially forged. `python-requests/2.34.2`, `Microsoft.Graph.Client/6.2.0` and `kiota-dotnet/1.17.1` are the values Unit 42 observed, not a signature - an operator who reads this report will set a browser user-agent in the next campaign. Treat the client string as one feature among several and never as the sole discriminator; the volumetric and breadth features are what survive the operator changing one line of code.

  Conversely, SDK client strings are overwhelmingly benign in isolation. `kiota-dotnet` and `Microsoft.Graph.Client` are Microsoft's own generated SDK stacks and appear across a huge amount of first-party and vendor tooling. Alerting on their presence alone produces nothing but noise. Their value here is conditional - an SDK string on a principal that has never previously presented one is the finding.

  Genuine bulk human activity exists and will score. Departing employees downloading their own OneDrive, a team archiving a project site before a reorganisation, or a new starter syncing everything on day one all produce one-off spikes. Sustained rate over many minutes, breadth across resources the principal does not own, and a client string change are what separate these.

  Migration and tenant-merger windows break the baseline wholesale. During a migration, thousands of principals legitimately change shape at once. Suppress the model for the migration window and the affected principals explicitly rather than letting it retrain on the anomaly, or it will absorb the new shape and go quiet.

  Low-volume targeted theft evades this hunt by design. Unit 42 notes the actors sought individuals responsible for high-value files rather than generic mailboxes, so an operator who takes twenty specific documents will not register volumetrically at all. This hunt catches the bulk-collection mode; the identity-side hunts catch the access that precedes both modes.
notes: |
  Feature construction, per principal per hour. Build these from unified audit log and `CloudAppEvents` records, keyed on `AccountObjectId` rather than display name so a renamed account does not reset its own history.

  - Request rate - count of `FileAccessed`, `FileDownloaded` and `FileSyncDownloadedFull` operations per minute, and the maximum sustained one-minute rate inside the hour.
  - Breadth - distinct `SiteUrl`, distinct document libraries, and distinct owning principals whose content was read. Breadth across owners is the strongest single feature, because ordinary work is narrow in ownership even when it is high in volume.
  - Client shape - the user-agent or client string, plus whether this principal has ever presented that string before. A first-ever SDK or scripting string on a human principal is a step change, not a gradient.
  - Egress shape - distinct `ClientIP` values and their autonomous systems, and whether the address space is hosting, VPN or residential proxy. Unit 42 states these actors typically route through residential proxy networks and commercial VPNs, which is why hosting-only reputation lists miss them.
  - Read-to-write ratio against the principal's own history - a principal that has historically written and read a library in rough proportion and now reads it hundreds of times with no writes is behaving like a collector rather than a user.
  - Search behaviour - `SearchQueryPerformed` volume and term breadth immediately preceding a download burst. Unit 42 describes systematic targeting of high-value files, which implies discovery before collection.

  Scoring. Fit per-principal baselines over at least 30 days and score the current window against the principal's own distribution, not against a tenant-wide mean - a tenant mean is dominated by service principals and will hide every human account. A robust z-score on log-transformed counts, or a median-absolute-deviation score, handles the heavy right tail better than a standard deviation on raw counts. Require at least two independent features to deviate before alerting; rate alone is the noisiest feature in the set and breadth-across-owners the cleanest.

  A worked KQL starting point for the client-string step change, which is the cheapest high-yield query in the hunt.

      let baseline = CloudAppEvents
        | where Timestamp between (ago(30d) .. ago(1d))
        | extend UA = tostring(RawEventData.UserAgent)
        | summarize KnownUAs = make_set(UA, 100) by AccountObjectId;
      CloudAppEvents
      | where Timestamp > ago(1d)
      | where ActionType in ("FileAccessed", "FileDownloaded", "FileSyncDownloadedFull")
      | extend UA = tostring(RawEventData.UserAgent)
      | summarize Ops = count(), Sites = dcount(tostring(RawEventData.SiteUrl)),
                  IPs = dcount(tostring(RawEventData.ClientIP))
                by AccountObjectId, UA, bin(Timestamp, 1h)
      | join kind=inner baseline on AccountObjectId
      | where UA !in (KnownUAs)
      | where Ops > 500
      | project Timestamp, AccountObjectId, UA, Ops, Sites, IPs
      | order by Ops desc

  Both lookbacks are inside the 30-day Defender advanced hunting retention, and every `let` is used. The join is on `AccountObjectId` with the baseline window deliberately ending at `ago(1d)` so today's activity cannot contaminate the set it is being compared against - a baseline that includes the window under test will absorb the anomaly and the query will return nothing. The `Ops > 500` floor is a starting value to keep the first run readable, not a tuned threshold; derive the real floor from your own per-principal distribution.

  On the exfiltration leg. Unit 42 records three routes out - open-source tools such as Rclone and cloud storage platforms, native SaaS and REST APIs or scripted HTTP-based exfiltration, and SaaS-to-SaaS transfers. The third is the one this hunt is positioned to see and perimeter controls are not, because the data never traverses a corporate egress point at all. A principal reading at machine rate and a sanctioned SaaS connector writing at the same rate in the same window is the SaaS-to-SaaS shape.

  Chain context for triage, in Unit 42 order - vishing while posing as internal IT support, an adversary-in-the-middle proxy capturing credentials and MFA approvals in real time, unauthorised MFA device enrolment within minutes of authenticating, then this collection stage, then extortion messages sent internally by email and Microsoft Teams from a compromised account pointing at a Tor-hosted negotiation site. A volumetric hit here should trigger a backward look at device-registration and sign-in events for the same principal in the preceding hours, and a forward look at internal messaging from that account.

  Unit 42 does not say how the scripts authenticated - whether they carried a replayed session cookie, a refresh token, or a token minted through an authorised third-party OAuth application. The report lists authorising unapproved third-party OAuth applications or data-sync utilities among the persistence techniques observed across these actors, so an application-level grant is plausible, but the report does not attest which mechanism fed the scripted clients. Check both consent grants and session state on a hit.

  Scope note on existing coverage. A grep for `Microsoft.Graph.Client` and for `Graph SDK` across all hunt bodies returns nothing. The adjacent hunts cover earlier links in the same chain and are deliberately not duplicated here: H302 covers an automated token-replay engine requesting fresh access tokens over default OAuth flows with a scripted HTTP client, which is the token stage rather than the collection stage; H411 covers replay of a captured session or refresh token from attacker infrastructure so no new MFA prompt appears; H329 covers the added MFA device that follows a passkey-themed AiTM page. This hunt starts after all three have happened and scores what the operator then does with the access. M046 and M042 touch scripted cloud clients volumetrically but key on device-registration and authentication events rather than on file and mail collection breadth.

# M075

The quiet part of a Com intrusion is the part that moves tens of millions of records.

Unit 42's write-up of Com-affiliated vishing activity tracks the loud stages carefully — the phone call from someone claiming to be IT, the adversary-in-the-middle page that relays the MFA approval in real time, the attacker's own phone enrolled as a second factor within minutes. Then the report says something easy to read past. One cluster automated exfiltration with Python scripts presenting a `python-httpx` user agent against mailbox, OneDrive and SharePoint content. Another used `python-requests/2.34.2` and the Microsoft Graph SDK against SharePoint and OneDrive. In some cases the haul ran to tens of millions of CRM and recruiting records.

There is no threshold that catches that and not the backup agent. A records-management service principal reads more before lunch than a partner at a law firm reads in a year, and both are correct. The only stable reference point is the principal's own history: what it normally reads, how fast, how widely, whose content, and with which client.

That makes this a modelling problem rather than a rule. The features that matter are breadth across owners, sustained rate, and a client string the principal has never presented before — and the last of those is a step change, not a slope, which is why it is the cheapest place to start.

## Why

- A static threshold cannot work here and a per-principal one can. The legitimate range of cloud file-access volume inside one tenant spans several orders of magnitude, so any tenant-wide number is either blind to humans or drowning in service principals. Scoring each principal against its own distribution is the only framing that survives contact with a real estate.
- The collection stage is where the damage is decided, and it is downstream of every identity control that already failed. By this point the vishing call worked, the AiTM proxy relayed the MFA approval, and an attacker device is enrolled. Volumetric collection modelling is the layer that still has something to say after authentication has been fully defeated.
- SaaS-to-SaaS transfer never crosses a corporate egress point. Unit 42 lists it as one of three exfiltration routes, and it is invisible to proxy and DLP controls watching the perimeter. Audit-log volumetrics are where that route is observable at all.
- A first-ever SDK client string on a human principal is a genuinely sharp feature. `kiota-dotnet` and `Microsoft.Graph.Client` are Microsoft's own generated stacks and are meaningless in isolation, but a lawyer's account that has presented a browser for two years and now presents a .NET SDK has changed category, not degree.
- The operator can change the user agent and cannot easily change the shape. Rate, breadth across content owners, and a read-to-write ratio that inverts are properties of bulk collection itself. Building the hunt on those and treating the published strings as one weak feature is what keeps it working after this report is public.
