# M060

The attacker made no attempt to be quiet, and it still took months to find them.

That is the part of Unit 42's case worth sitting with. A European bank discovered unauthorised VPN access attempts spanning several months. The intrusion started at a third-party-managed external gateway appliance "that was not properly configured to enforce strict authentication" — in practice, missing MFA on remote access — and from there the attacker simply swept. Unit 42 describes "extensive host and network scans across a wide range of protocols and interacting with thousands of systems", "multiple password-guessing campaigns" against administrator accounts, and automated web requests scraping internal documentation and enumerating network shares.

Their verdict on the operator is unflattering: the activity "did not match the behavior typically associated with a sophisticated threat actor", and "Their actions showed little effort to remain hidden or avoid detection."

So why did it work for months?

Because none of it was remarkable one event at a time. A connection to a host is not an incident. A failed login is not an incident. A wiki page fetch is not an incident. The signal was never in any individual record; it was in how *many kinds* of things one identity was doing, and no static threshold can express that in a bank where some accounts legitimately touch thousands of systems and most touch five.

Which is what makes this a modelling problem rather than a rule.

The instinct is to threshold on volume — alert when an identity exceeds N connections. That fails in both directions. It fires constantly on vulnerability scanners, backup agents and monitoring systems, and it misses the administrator whose normal day is already above N. Tune it per environment and it rots the moment the environment changes.

Score breadth instead, and score it relative to each identity's own history.

Five terms, computed per authenticated remote identity per day. Destination fan-out — how many distinct internal hosts. Protocol breadth — how many distinct ports, which is the term that catches scanning specifically, because a legitimate user reaches many hosts on *few* ports while a scanner reaches them across many. Failed-to-successful authentication ratio, which catches the password-guessing campaigns. Share-enumeration count. And documentation retrieval rate, for the scraping.

Then compare each to that identity's own trailing median rather than to a fleet-wide number. An administrator who touches three hundred hosts every week is unremarkable at three hundred and twenty and very interesting at four thousand. The same model handles both without anyone tuning a threshold for their role.

Two refinements that matter more than they look.

Normalise against the daily fleet median before scoring. Password-policy changes and certificate expiries produce failure spikes across *many* identities at once; a real credential attack is concentrated in one. Dividing out the fleet median makes that distinction automatic instead of a Monday-morning triage conversation.

And keep the scanner roster as a named deliverable with owners, not as a tuning exclusion buried in a query. Vulnerability management, asset discovery, monitoring and backup all behave like this legitimately. The point of writing them down is that an account behaving like a scanner *while not being on the list* is precisely the finding.

Where the case ended is a reminder of what the score is protecting. After months of sweeping, the intruder went for a WSUS remote code execution vulnerability on the central configuration management server — "This server handled software distribution across the bank, making it a prime target for privilege escalation and long-term persistence." Cortex XDR caught the code execution and contained it. Every month of reconnaissance before that point was scoreable and unscored.

Be honest about the limits. This model watches remote-access identities, so an attacker who establishes an on-premises foothold drops out of its view. And it is tuned for a loud operator — a patient one pacing the same activity across months would score low, and a low score would be measuring the model's blind spot rather than the absence of an intruder.

## Modelling approach

**Unit of analysis.** One authenticated remote identity per day. Resolve the VPN-assigned internal address to the authenticated identity for the duration of each session, since every network-layer metric is attributed through that mapping.

**Features, computed per identity-day.**

- `fanout` — count of distinct internal destination addresses contacted.
- `port_breadth` — count of distinct destination ports contacted. This is the scanning term. A legitimate user reaches many hosts over few ports; a sweep reaches them over many.
- `auth_fail_ratio` — failed authentications divided by failed plus successful, across events 4625, 4624 and 4776, attributed to the identity and to its assigned address.
- `admin_target_rate` — the share of authentication attempts whose target account holds a privileged role, since Unit 42 notes the campaigns focused on administrator accounts.
- `share_enum` — count of distinct network shares accessed or enumerated, from events 5140 and 5145.
- `doc_rate` — count of distinct documentation pages and attachments retrieved from internal repositories.
- `session_hours` — total connected time, as the denominator that distinguishes a sustained low rate from a short burst.

**Scoring.** For each feature, compute a robust z-score against the identity's own trailing 30-day distribution using median and median absolute deviation rather than mean and standard deviation, so a single prior bad day does not inflate the baseline. Then divide each by the same feature's daily fleet median to cancel environment-wide events. The composite is the sum of the clipped per-feature scores, weighted toward `port_breadth` and `auth_fail_ratio`, which are the two terms with the fewest benign explanations.

**Cold start.** An identity with fewer than 14 days of history has no usable self-baseline. Score these against the median of their peer group — same role, same department, same access tier — and mark the score as provisional. New starters and newly provisioned service accounts both land here, and both are worth looking at anyway.

**Output.** A daily ranked list, not an alert. The top of the list is a review queue; the value is in the ranking being stable enough that a new name near the top is itself informative.

**Validation.** Replay the model over a period containing a known authorised penetration test or red-team engagement. If the engagement does not surface near the top of the ranking, the weighting is wrong. This is the cheapest available ground truth and most organisations already have some.

## Why

- **The activity was loud and still invisible for months.** No individual event crossed any threshold, which is the defining property of a problem that needs scoring rather than rules.
- **Static thresholds cannot work across a mixed estate.** Scanners and backup agents sit permanently above any useful line while most identities sit permanently below it, so one number cannot serve both.
- **Breadth discriminates where volume does not.** Distinct-port count separates a sweep from a busy administrator far more cleanly than connection count, because legitimate breadth is wide in hosts and narrow in protocols.
- **Self-relative baselining removes per-role tuning.** Comparing each identity to its own trailing history means an administrator with a genuinely broad remit needs no exception entry.
- **Fleet-median normalisation cancels estate-wide events.** Password-policy changes and certificate expiries spike failures across many identities; a credential attack concentrates in one.
- **The scanner roster is a deliverable, not an exclusion.** Writing down which accounts may legitimately behave this way turns every unlisted scanner-shaped identity into a finding.
- **Reconnaissance was the long phase, and the only one with room to act.** Months of sweeping preceded a single exploitation attempt against the configuration management server, so the scoreable window was wide.
- **Third-party-managed edge infrastructure is where this started.** A gateway outside the organisation's own configuration control failed to enforce strict authentication, which is a scoping question as much as a detection one.
- **The model's blind spots are specific and statable.** It sees remote-access identities and favours loud operators; naming both is what keeps a low score from being read as assurance.

## Existing HEARTH coverage and the distinction

[[M025]] is the closest existing hunt and shares the core insight that scope rather than raw volume is the discriminator — it scores a source making an anomalous number of connection attempts against ONE authentication service as a credential flood. The distinction is dimensionality: M025 models a single behaviour against a single service, while this hunt composites five features across network, authentication, share and document telemetry for one remote identity, and scores each against that identity's own trailing history rather than a service-level norm. The password-guessing term here is roughly M025's question folded in as one input among several.

[[H109]] covers an SMB connection spike from a single host to many endpoints, and [[H111]] covers deployment of named port-scanning tools. Both are rule-shaped detections of the reconnaissance this model scores: H109 fixes on one protocol, H111 on named tooling, and neither generalises to an operator who sweeps slowly across many protocols with built-in utilities. [[H369]] covers password spraying with EBurst specifically, which is the tool-named version of this hunt's authentication-ratio feature.
