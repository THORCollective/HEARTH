hypothesis: >-
  A host that lies about its workload cannot lie about its electricity. When a monitoring interface is subverted, the reported utilization series decouples from every measurement taken outside that interface - chassis and rail power, inlet and exhaust temperature, fan speed, and the scheduler's record of what was allocated to the card. No single reading proves anything, because idle GPUs are normal and noisy sensors are normal, but the relationship between reported load and physical draw is stable per host and per model of card, and it is that relationship that breaks. Model the expected power and thermal profile as a function of reported utilization and allocated workload on a per-host, per-card basis, then rank hosts by how badly the residual departs from their own learned baseline over a sustained window.

false_positive_notes: |
  This hunt deliberately tolerates a wide band, because the physical series are noisy and the honest relationship between reported load and power is looser than it looks.
  - Idle cards drawing real power is normal. A GPU at 0% utilization still draws tens of watts at idle, and that floor varies by card model, power limit and driver version. Model within card family and learn the floor per host rather than assuming a datasheet value.
  - Reported utilization is a poor proxy for power even when nobody is lying. The utilization figure reports whether kernels were resident, not how much of the die was busy, so a memory-bound workload can report high utilization at low power and a dense matrix workload the reverse. This is the main source of residual noise and the reason the hunt looks for sustained divergence rather than per-interval outliers.
  - Shared hosts break the allocation signal. If a card is shared through time-slicing, MIG or MPS, or if researchers run work outside the scheduler, the scheduler record understates the legitimate workload and the host looks like it is doing unexplained work. Establish which hosts allow off-scheduler use before ranking them, and treat those hosts as a separate population.
  - Thermal series lag and are contaminated by neighbours. Inlet temperature reflects the rack and the room, not only this chassis, and exhaust and fan response lag load by minutes. Use power as the primary out-of-band channel and thermal as corroboration, with lag allowed for in the model.
  - BMC and PDU data is frequently coarse or stale. Sampling intervals of a minute or more, averaged readings, and per-outlet rather than per-component granularity are all common. Coarse data still works for sustained divergence; it will not support a short-window rule, which is another reason the window is long.
  - A ranked host is a question, not a verdict. Benchmarking, burn-in, firmware updates, driver regressions and a genuinely busy off-scheduler researcher all land in the same bucket. The output of this hunt is an ordered worklist whose top entries get the on-host provenance check in [[H464]], which is where the answer actually is.
notes: |
  Source reporting is Elastic Security Labs' November 12, 2026 analysis of REF3364. Elastic reports that the modified NVIDIA management library "forwards queries to the genuine NVIDIA library, then alters selected results", that "GPU utilization is reported as 0%", and that temperature and power "are replaced with random readings of 36 to 46 degrees Celsius and 100 to 200 watts". This hunt exists because of that last detail: the fabricated values are randomised within a plausible band specifically so that a time series looks ordinary, which defeats a threshold rule and leaves a statistical approach as the usable one. The deterministic, host-side version of this detection is [[H464]]; the initial-access route in the same intrusion is [[H465]]. The closest existing model-assisted hunt is [[M040]], which covers cryptomining resource abuse, but it reasons from host-reported resource consumption - the series this hunt treats as untrusted.
  Why this is Alchemy and not Flames - Three of the four Alchemy triggers apply. The behaviour is detectable by anomaly rather than signature, because the component was built to produce ordinary-looking readings and there is no value or string to match on. A static threshold does not generalise, because the honest relationship between reported utilization and power draw depends on card model, power limit, driver version and workload mix, and differs per host. And the detection requires per-host volumetric baselining rather than a rule: the finding is a residual that departs from this host's own learned behaviour, which cannot be expressed without first learning it.
  Feature construction - Work at a fixed interval, five or fifteen minutes depending on the coarsest input, and build per card where card-level data exists and per chassis where only chassis power is available. Per interval and per host assemble: mean and maximum reported utilization; reported GPU power and temperature; out-of-band chassis or rail power; inlet and exhaust temperature; fan RPM; allocated-workload indicators from the scheduler (jobs resident, requested GPUs, requesting user); host CPU utilization and network bytes in and out. Carry card model, power limit and driver version as grouping keys, because the power-per-utilization relationship is only comparable within a model family.
  The model - Fit expected out-of-band power as a function of reported utilization, reported GPU power, allocated workload and ambient inlet temperature, per host, over a training window of at least 30 days of ordinary operation. A simple approach is sufficient and preferable to a complex one here: a robust regression or a quantile model of out-of-band power conditioned on reported utilization, which yields a residual per interval. Rank hosts by the fraction of intervals in a trailing window whose residual exceeds the host's own high quantile, and require persistence - the behaviour being hunted runs for days, so a host that only diverges for one interval is sensor noise. Allow lag on the thermal terms, since exhaust and fan response trail load.
  What the divergence looks like in this case - The reported series is pinned low and smooth while the out-of-band series carries a real sustained load. The specific reported values Elastic documents give the expected shape: utilization at zero, GPU power reported in a 100 to 200 watt band, temperature reported in a 36 to 46 degree band, all with randomised variation and none of it correlated with the physical measurements. The signature is therefore not a high number anywhere - it is the collapse of correlation between two series that are normally tightly coupled, with the in-band series losing its variance structure.
  A second, cheaper discriminator - Variance, not level. A genuine idle card's reported temperature and power track ambient and workload, so they move together and move with the rest of the rack. Fabricated values drawn from a fixed band do neither: they vary within a narrow range independently of ambient inlet temperature and independently of the neighbouring cards in the same chassis. Testing for loss of cross-correlation between a card's reported thermal series and the chassis inlet series is a low-cost check that needs no out-of-band power at all, which matters in estates where PDU and BMC collection is not in place. Expected, not observed by Elastic: this specific discriminator - the report documents the randomised bands but does not propose a detection method, and the correlation test is inferred from the described behaviour.
  Third signal, allocation versus consumption - Independent of any physical sensor, compare the scheduler's record of what was allocated against what the host claims to have done. A card that the scheduler shows as unallocated for days, on a host whose own GPU telemetry also reports it idle, but whose chassis draws load, is the clean case. The inverse - a card the scheduler shows as heavily allocated while the host reports it idle - is also worth ranking, because it is what a subverted interface looks like underneath a legitimate workload sharing the same card.
  Deliverable - A ranked list of hosts with, for each: the trailing-window divergence fraction, the reported and out-of-band series plotted together over the window, the scheduler allocation for the same window, and the card model and driver version. The top entries go straight to the host-side provenance check - whether any installed package owns the GPU management library, and whether a dot-prefixed backup of it exists beside it - which is [[H464]] and which converts a ranking into a finding or clears it in minutes.
  Scope and prerequisites - This hunt is only possible where at least one measurement channel bypasses the host operating system. In an owned data centre that is the BMC and the PDU. In a colocation facility it may be per-outlet metering only. In public cloud, per-instance power is not exposed, so the usable substitutes are the scheduler-allocation comparison, the loss-of-correlation test on the reported series, and billed-versus-useful-work accounting; state which channel is available before building, because the model's power term is otherwise unavailable and the hunt reduces to its allocation and variance components.

# M070

The library lied about utilization. It could not lie about the power supply.

Elastic Security Labs documented a GPU host where the NVIDIA management library had been replaced with a shim that forwarded every call to the real library and then edited the answer. Utilization came back as 0%. Temperature came back as a random value between 36 and 46 degrees. Power came back as a random value between 100 and 200 watts.

Read that last part again, because it is what makes this a modelling problem rather than a rule. The operator did not zero the readings. Zeroes stand out. They chose a narrow, believable band and randomised within it, so that anyone watching a graph would see a lightly loaded, perfectly ordinary card. A threshold has nothing to fire on.

What the shim could not touch is anything measured outside the host. The chassis still drew the current the GPU actually consumed. The inlet and exhaust sensors still responded to real heat. The fans still spun up. The scheduler still knew that nobody had been allocated that card in a week.

So the hunt is not "find a busy GPU". It is: learn, per host and per card model, how reported load normally relates to physical draw and allocated work — then find the hosts where that relationship has come apart and stayed apart.

There is a second tell that needs no power data at all. A real idle card's reported temperature drifts with the room and with its neighbours in the same chassis. Numbers drawn from a fixed band do not. They vary, but they vary alone.

## Why

- **The fabricated values were designed to survive being looked at.** Randomising inside a plausible band is an explicit accommodation to monitoring, and it is why a static threshold on reported utilization cannot work. That pushes the detection toward a learned relationship, which is the definition of a model-assisted hunt rather than a rule.
- **The relationship between reported load and real power is stable, and it is stable per host.** Within a card model, at a fixed power limit, power draw tracks real work closely enough to predict. That makes the residual meaningful — and it also makes a global threshold useless, because the honest curve differs by card, power limit, driver and workload mix.
- **Out-of-band telemetry is the only channel the host cannot edit.** Every in-band reading on that machine passed through a compromised library, and the process listings passed through a kernel rootkit from the same toolkit. The BMC, the PDU and the scheduler are outside both. On a host like this, they are not corroboration — they are the only measurements that were ever true.
- **Scheduler records give a free, sensor-independent version of the same question.** Comparing what was allocated against what the host claims it did needs no physical instrumentation and exists in every managed cluster. It is the component of this hunt that still works in public cloud, where per-instance power is not exposed.
- **The output is a worklist, and the answer is one command away.** Ranking hosts by divergence does not identify the cause — benchmarking, driver regressions and an off-scheduler researcher all look similar. But the top entries resolve immediately against an on-host provenance check: does any installed package own the GPU management library, and is there a hidden copy of the real one beside it. Statistics narrow the estate; the file system settles it.
