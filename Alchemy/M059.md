title: Modelling per-source log silence to find the appliance whose telemetry stopped for exactly one session
hypothesis: >-
  An attacker with root on an appliance deletes or rewrites its logs, and the evidence of that is not in
  the logs - it is in their absence. Mandiant's detection guidance after the ARMORY CASTLE intrusion names
  the signal directly: "Anti-forensics: gaps in syslog forwarding from the appliance; an interruption
  bounded by a single session is a strong indicator of log tampering." The difficulty is that a fixed
  silence threshold cannot work across an estate, because every log source has a different natural rate -
  a busy appliance emits thousands of events an hour and a standby one legitimately emits none for a
  whole night, so any single threshold either drowns in false findings or misses the quiet sources
  entirely. Model each forwarding source against its OWN rolling, time-of-day-aware event rate, score
  silence windows by how far they fall below that source's expectation, and rank the residue. Then apply
  the discriminator from the report - correlate each gap against session and administrative activity, and
  promote the gaps whose boundaries sit inside a single session.

false_positive_notes: |
  Telemetry gaps are common and almost all of them are boring, which is why this is a ranking analytic
  rather than an alert. The dominant benign causes need to be joined out before anything is escalated.
  Transport and platform failures are first - a collector restart, a relay crash, an expired TLS
  certificate, a full disk, a rate limit or a network outage each produce a clean silence window
  indistinguishable from tampering at the source. The giveaway is breadth: a gap that appears across many
  sources at the same moment is infrastructure, and the analytic should group by time bucket and suppress
  any window where an unusual proportion of sources went quiet together. Planned maintenance is second -
  reboots, firmware upgrades and patch windows legitimately silence a source, which is why the change
  calendar is a required input rather than a nice-to-have. Genuine quiet is third, and it is the reason a
  flat threshold fails: a standby appliance, a lab device or a site outside business hours may
  legitimately emit nothing for hours, so the model has to compare each source against its own
  time-of-day profile and should exclude buckets where the expected rate is too low to support a
  conclusion. Rotation and batching are fourth - a source that forwards on a schedule rather than
  continuously produces regular gaps by design, and those sources should be identified during tuning and
  modelled on their own cadence or excluded. Two cautions against over-suppression. Do not suppress a gap
  simply because a reboot is recorded nearby, since rebooting is also how an attacker clears state and
  the report's guidance is to preserve before acting. And do not require the gap to be long - the
  reported behaviour removed a targeted time window, so a short, precisely-bounded gap in a high-rate

  ALCHEMY ANALYTIC - per-source volumetric model over telemetry absence, not a static rule. Platform:
  SIEM-side, log-source agnostic, applied to appliances and any agentless device whose logs are both
  locally held and remotely forwarded. Driven by two reports from the same month that independently
  describe the same anti-forensic behaviour - Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group, "ARMORY
  CASTLE Exploits BeyondTrust" (15 October 2026), which supplies the detection guidance this analytic
  implements, and Volexity, "A Series of Unauthenticated Events: Chained Exploitation of Ivanti Connect

  the single-host confirmation hunt for a candidate this analytic produces.
  Why a model rather than a threshold. The quantity being measured is the absence of events, and the
  expected quantity differs by orders of magnitude between sources. A threshold set for a busy appliance
  misses a quiet one; a threshold set for a quiet one buries the analyst. Worse, the same source varies
  by time of day - a VPN concentrator at 09:00 and at 03:00 are different distributions. So the
  comparison has to be each source against its own history, bucketed by hour of day, with the deviation
  expressed in units of that source's own variability. That is the Alchemy framing and it is what makes
  the analytic portable across an estate without per-source tuning.
  What the two reports contribute. Mandiant observed ARMORY CASTLE "systematically deleting system, web
  server, and application-level logs" throughout the intrusion, including a single command issued with
  elevated privileges that removed the appliance authentication, SSL, HTTP access and error, PHP and
  system message logs together, plus site-specific httpd access and error logs and `/tmp/sshauth.*`. Their
  recommendation is to "Forward appliance syslog to a SIEM over TLS with retention that covers a
  multi-month exposure window, and collect reverse-proxy or WAF and perimeter firewall logs for the
  appliance's address", precisely "since an attacker with host access can delete on-appliance logs".
  Volexity's case shows the subtler variant - a payload that reads a log, filters out entries inside a
  targeted window, then truncates and rewrites the file with the remaining records, preserving older
  entries. Mandiant also notes a tooling detail relevant to scoring: the BARECLAW tunneler was run with
  `-q`, which suppresses its logging entirely, so some of the silence is a tool choosing not to speak
  rather than a log being edited after the fact.
  The discriminator, and why it is the valuable half. Gap detection alone produces a long list dominated
  by infrastructure. The report's phrasing is what turns it into an analytic worth running - "an
  interruption bounded by a single session is a strong indicator of log tampering". A gap that starts
  after a session begins and ends before it ends is not a transport failure, because transport failures do
  not respect session boundaries. The join against session and administrative activity is therefore not
  an enrichment step but the core of the scoring, and it is why the session records are a required input.
  Scoring model, stated concretely enough to implement.
  Bucket every forwarded event by source and by a 5-minute window over a 30-day lookback. For each
  source, compute the expected rate per hour-of-day as the median of that source's historical buckets for
  that hour, together with a robust dispersion measure such as the median absolute deviation. Discard
  hour-of-day cells whose expected rate is below a floor - roughly ten events per bucket - because a
  source that is normally near-silent cannot support an absence conclusion. For the remaining buckets,
  score each observed bucket as the number of dispersion units below expectation, and define a gap as a
  run of consecutive buckets scoring beyond that threshold - the run grouping is done after the per-bucket
  scoring above, by collapsing adjacent flagged buckets per source. Rank gaps by the product of their depth and
  duration, so a complete silence of thirty minutes in a high-rate source outranks a mild dip of six
  hours in a quiet one. Then apply three multiplicative adjustments. Suppress heavily where the same time
  bucket shows an unusual proportion of all sources going quiet together, which indicates platform
  failure. Suppress where the collector connection state shows a transport interruption for that source,
  or where the change calendar records maintenance. And promote strongly where the gap's start and end
  both fall inside a single support session or administrative login on that appliance. The output is a
  ranked list of source-and-window pairs for an analyst to confirm with [[H386]].
  Sample Splunk SPL for the per-source, hour-aware model. It builds the expectation from the source's own
  history and emits the deepest silence windows, rather than applying any global threshold.
  `index=appliance earliest=-30d | bucket _time span=5m | stats count AS events BY _time, host | eval hod=strftime(_time,"%H") | eventstats median(events) AS expected BY host, hod | eval dev=abs(events-expected) | eventstats median(dev) AS dispersion BY host, hod | where expected > 10 | eval depth=(expected-events)/(dispersion+1) | where depth > 3 | table _time, host, events, expected, dispersion, depth | sort 0 host, _time`
  Sample Splunk SPL for the estate-wide suppression, which must run alongside the query above. A time
  bucket in which many sources fell silent together is an infrastructure event and the per-source
  findings inside it should be discounted.
  `index=appliance earliest=-30d | bucket _time span=5m | stats dc(host) AS reporting_sources BY _time | eventstats median(reporting_sources) AS normal_sources | eval fraction_reporting=reporting_sources/normal_sources | where fraction_reporting < 0.7 | table _time, reporting_sources, normal_sources, fraction_reporting`
  Sample KQL for the same model over a custom appliance syslog table in Sentinel or Defender XDR. The join
  is bounded on hour-of-day so it compares like with like rather than correlating unrelated days, and the
  baseline window deliberately excludes the most recent day so current tampering cannot raise its own
  expectation.
  `let lookback = 30d; let baseline = ApplianceSyslog_CL | where TimeGenerated between (ago(lookback) .. ago(1d)) | summarize Events = count() by Computer, Bin = bin(TimeGenerated, 5m) | summarize Expected = percentile(Events, 50) by Computer, Hour = hourofday(Bin); let observed = ApplianceSyslog_CL | where TimeGenerated > ago(7d) | summarize Events = count() by Computer, Bin = bin(TimeGenerated, 5m) | extend Hour = hourofday(Bin); observed | join kind=inner baseline on Computer, Hour | where Expected > 10 and Events < Expected * 0.1 | project Bin, Computer, Events, Expected, Shortfall = Expected - Events | order by Shortfall desc`
  Sample KQL for the session-boundary promotion, which is the scoring step that separates this from
  generic gap monitoring. It keeps only gaps whose start and end both fall within one session window on
  the same appliance, using an explicit time bound on the join.
  `let gaps = ApplianceGaps_CL | where TimeGenerated > ago(7d) | project Computer, GapStart, GapEnd; let sessions = ApplianceSessions_CL | where TimeGenerated > ago(7d) | project Computer, SessionId, SessionStart, SessionEnd, SessionUser; gaps | join kind=inner sessions on Computer | where GapStart >= SessionStart and GapEnd <= SessionEnd | project Computer, SessionId, SessionUser, SessionStart, GapStart, GapEnd, SessionEnd | order by GapStart asc`
  Tuning guidance. Start with the dispersion threshold and the expected-rate floor rather than the gap
  duration, because duration is the parameter most likely to hide the reported behaviour - a selective
  rewrite removes a targeted window, which may be minutes. Expect the first run to be dominated by
  collector and relay problems, and treat that as a useful finding in itself: a source that silently
  stopped forwarding weeks ago is an unmonitored appliance regardless of whether anyone attacked it. Once
  the transport joins are wired in, the residue should be small enough to read by hand.
  What to do with a ranked candidate. Confirm with [[H386]], which compares the forwarded copy against the
  log still on the appliance and checks for rotation, upgrade and disk-pressure explanations. Where
  tampering is confirmed, both reports converge on the same response - preserve before acting. Mandiant
  advises snapshotting virtual appliances with memory and exporting the disk before any change or reboot,
  and Volexity notes that the Ivanti Integrity Checker Tool may not identify a compromised device because
  the chain patched memory and restored configuration rather than modifying disk.
  Existing HEARTH coverage and the distinction. [[M025]] is the closest existing analytic in shape, scoring
  anomalous connection volume against ONE authentication service to find a brute-force or spray, and
  [[M023]] and [[M024]] model per-entity authentication and RDP fan-out. All three score the PRESENCE of
  unusual volume. This one scores its ABSENCE, which is a different measurement with different failure
  modes - transport reliability, rotation cadence and genuinely quiet sources - and a different
  discriminator in the session-boundary correlation. [[H386]] is the per-host confirmation hunt, and

# M059

The evidence of log tampering is not in the logs. It is in the hole where they used to be.

Mandiant put the signal in one sentence in their ARMORY CASTLE detection guidance: "gaps in syslog forwarding from the appliance; an interruption bounded by a single session is a strong indicator of log tampering." That second clause is the whole analytic. Gaps are everywhere and nearly all of them are a collector restart. A gap that begins after someone connects and ends before they disconnect is a different kind of object.

Why this has to be a model rather than a threshold is a question of units. A busy VPN concentrator emits thousands of events an hour. A standby appliance legitimately emits nothing all night. Set one silence threshold across an estate and it either buries the analyst in quiet-source findings or never fires on the loud ones. The same source is not even stable against itself — 09:00 and 03:00 are different distributions. So the comparison is each source against its own rolling, hour-of-day-aware rate, with the shortfall measured in units of that source's own variability, and buckets where the expected rate is too low to support any conclusion simply discarded.

Two reports from the same month describe why the behaviour is worth modelling, and they describe two different versions of it.

ARMORY CASTLE deleted logs wholesale — one elevated command removed appliance authentication, SSL, HTTP access and error, PHP and system message logs together, along with site httpd logs and `/tmp/sshauth.*`. UTA0576, on the Ivanti appliance Volexity investigated, did the careful version: read the log, filter out the entries inside a chosen window, rewrite the file with the rest. Months of history preserved, size plausible, only the operator's own window missing.

That second case sets the tuning parameter that matters most. Do not reach for gap duration first. A selective rewrite removes a *targeted window*, which may be minutes long. A short, precisely bounded gap in a high-rate source is a far stronger signal than a long drift in a quiet one, and ranking by depth multiplied by duration gets that ordering right where a minimum-duration filter would hide exactly the reported behaviour.

The suppression work is what makes the output readable, and it is mostly joins rather than cleverness. Group by time bucket and discount any window where an unusual share of all sources fell quiet together — that is the collector, not an intrusion. Join the relay connection state, because an expired TLS certificate produces a perfect imitation of tampering. Join the change calendar, because reboots and firmware upgrades are silence with a ticket.

Then promote on the session boundary, and accept that the first run will be dominated by infrastructure problems anyway. That is worth having. A source that quietly stopped forwarding three weeks ago is an unmonitored appliance whether or not anyone ever attacked it, and finding those is a legitimate first-week outcome.

Two cautions against suppressing too eagerly. A nearby reboot record is not an exoneration — rebooting is also how state gets cleared, and both reports insist on preserving before acting. And some silence is a tool rather than an edit: BARECLAW was run with `-q`, which suppresses its logging entirely, so the absence was by design and never written in the first place.

A ranked candidate from this analytic is not a conclusion. It goes to [[H386]], which compares what reached the SIEM against what remains on the box and rules out rotation, upgrade and disk pressure.

## Why

- **Absence is the only artefact a root-level attacker cannot curate.** They can rewrite any log on the appliance, but not the fact that nothing arrived at the collector during the window they edited.
- **The report supplies a discriminator that makes gap detection worth running.** A gap bounded on both sides by a single session is not a transport failure, because transport failures do not respect session boundaries.
- **A single silence threshold cannot work across an estate, which is why this is modelled.** Event rates differ by orders of magnitude between sources and by time of day within one source, so the comparison must be per-source and hour-aware.
- **Ranking by depth and duration together catches the selective rewrite.** A minimum-duration filter would systematically hide the targeted-window tampering Volexity documented, which may span only minutes.
- **The benign causes are enumerable and joinable.** Collector failures, maintenance windows, scheduled batch forwarding and genuine quiet hours are all knowable inputs, which keeps the residue small without weakening the model.
- **Estate-wide correlation separates platform problems from intrusions cheaply.** If many sources go quiet in the same bucket, it is the pipeline, and discounting that window costs one aggregation.
- **It runs on index metadata, not event content.** Counting events per source per bucket needs no parser and no field extraction, so it works uniformly across appliance log formats.
- **Appliances are the intended target because they are agentless and attacker-writable.** No EDR, no file integrity monitoring, and a log set the root user controls — the forwarded stream is the only trustworthy record.
- **The first-run noise is itself a finding worth having.** Sources that stopped forwarding silently weeks ago are unmonitored devices, and this analytic surfaces them as a by-product.
- **It measures the inverse of HEARTH's existing volumetric analytics.** [[M025]], [[M023]] and [[M024]] score unusual presence of authentication or lateral-movement volume; this scores unusual absence, with different failure modes and a different correlation key.
