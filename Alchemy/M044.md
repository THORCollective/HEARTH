false_positive_notes: |
  Signer rarity has a substantial benign long tail and the model must be built expecting it, or the top of the ranking will be permanently occupied by legitimate niche software. The largest benign population is simply small-vendor and in-house software: a specialist engineering tool, a departmental line-of-business application, an internal utility signed with the organisation's own certificate, or a driver for unusual hardware will each appear on a handful of hosts and score as rare. These are resolved by dispositioning the SIGNER once rather than the binary every time, which is why the allowlist must be keyed on signing identity plus expected host population. A second population is new legitimate software arriving in the estate — every genuine vendor is first-seen once, so novelty alone must carry only moderate weight and should always be combined with mismatch. Expect a recurring spike whenever a vendor rotates its signing certificate, which changes the thumbprint and serial while the publisher name stays the same; score on publisher identity primarily and thumbprint secondarily so a routine rotation does not read as a new signer. Developer machines produce self-signed and test-signed binaries constantly and should be scoped by role. On the mismatch dimension, be careful with legitimate OEM and bundled software: a Lenovo-signed utility on a Lenovo laptop is entirely expected, and it is only the combination of signer with an implausible location, an implausible function, or an implausible host that carries signal — a Lenovo-signed binary running from `%TEMP%` on a Dell fleet is a different proposition. Finally, a valid signature on a REVOKED or expired certificate deserves separate handling: Windows may still execute it, and it is a strong indicator that belongs at the top of the ranking regardless of rarity.
notes: |
  Model shape - a per-execution score over four weighted inputs, surfaced as a ranked list rather than as alerts. (1) SIGNER RARITY - the number of distinct hosts in the fleet that have executed anything signed by this publisher over 90 days, scored inversely. (2) SIGNER NOVELTY - days since this publisher was first seen anywhere in the fleet, with a sharp weight inside the first 7 days. (3) SIGNER-TO-PURPOSE MISMATCH - whether the binary's install path, filename, and observed behaviour are consistent with the signer's actual product line; concretely, score a mismatch when a hardware-vendor signer runs from a user-writable path, when the signer's brand does not appear in the file's `Company`/`Product` resources, or when the host's hardware vendor does not match an OEM signer. (4) SIGNATURE STATE - valid, expired, revoked, or valid-but-countersigned-after-certificate-compromise, with revoked and expired scoring heavily. Multiply the result by a role weight from the asset inventory so that engineering hosts, which legitimately produce signing diversity, do not dominate.
  Reported basis from the source intel - the Google Doc campaign used THREE separate stolen code-signing certificates in one chain: one belonging to a Norwegian company (used on the fake "Google API Connector" ClickOnce application), one belonging to Lenovo (used on the TLS interception proxy disguised as a driver package, see [[H314]]), and one belonging to Discord Inc. (used on a fake DocSend desktop installer, where signature validation actually FAILED). That last case is instructive for tuning - a recognisable corporate signer name with a failing validation is both very high-signal and easy to miss if telemetry stores only a signed/unsigned boolean.
  The mismatch dimension is where most of the precision lives - rarity alone is noisy because legitimate niche software is rare too. Mismatch is much harder for an adversary to avoid, because they must choose between signing with a certificate whose owner plausibly ships software like theirs (a small pool, and one that often does not match the behaviour they need) or accepting an obvious inconsistency. Concretely useful mismatch features - an OEM or hardware-vendor signer on a binary outside `%PROGRAMFILES%`; a signer whose brand is absent from the binary's own version resources; a signer whose products are not otherwise present on the host; and a signer appearing on a host population that does not correspond to any inventoried software deployment.
  Correlating conditions that promote a ranked entry to an incident - the signed binary writes to `~/Library/LaunchAgents`, `HKLM\...\Run`, the root certificate store, or a service registration; the signed binary is a kernel driver (hand that case to [[M045]], which models driver loads specifically); the signed binary hollows or injects into another process; or the signer appears on fewer hosts than the number of security incidents it is associated with.
  Output shape and triage - a ranked list carrying, per entry: signer common name, certificate thumbprint and serial, validity dates, signature status, fleet host count, signer first-seen date, the binary's path and hashes, its version resources, and the mismatch features that fired. The fastest triage step is checking whether the signer ships any product the organisation actually licenses or has installed; a signer with no inventory presence and no procurement record is the shape being hunted.
  Deliverable that outlasts the hunt - a SIGNER REGISTER: every code-signing identity observed executing in the estate, its expected host population, and a disposition. Once that register exists, a new signer becomes a one-line diff, which is the same property that makes [[B048]] work for certificate trust.
  Why this is Alchemy rather than Flames - the binary is genuinely, verifiably signed, so there is no signature, hash, path or command line that generalises; a rule would either fire on all rare software or on none. The discriminating signal exists only as a relative measure of how a signing identity is distributed across a particular fleet compared with every other signing identity in it, which requires a per-environment model.

# M044

Huntress's Google Doc campaign analysis contains a detail worth modelling on its own: the operators used three separate stolen code-signing certificates in a single intrusion chain — one from a Norwegian company on the fake "Google API Connector", one from Lenovo on the TLS interception proxy disguised as a driver package, and one from Discord Inc. on a fake DocSend installer. Code signing is supposed to answer "who made this", and a stolen certificate turns that answer into an asset the adversary controls. Every downstream control inherits the lie: Authenticode validates, the publisher reads as a recognisable brand, reputation systems soften, and allowlisting policies written as "permit binaries signed by a trusted publisher" admit the payload by design. Treating signature validity as a security decision therefore fails silently and completely. What the adversary cannot forge is fit — a stolen certificate lands on a few hosts, signs something the real company does not make, runs from somewhere the real company would not install, and shows up in the fleet for the first time that week.

## Why

- **A stolen certificate inverts the control rather than evading it.** This is not a gap in signature checking; it is signature checking working correctly on a fraudulent input. Any detection strategy that consumes the signed/unsigned boolean will confirm the malware, which means the boolean has to be replaced by something the adversary does not control — and distribution across a fleet is exactly that.
- **Most telemetry pipelines discard the field the model needs.** Endpoint logging very commonly reduces Authenticode to a boolean and drops the publisher name, thumbprint and validity dates. Building this model forces those fields into retention, and that change pays off across unrelated investigations — the Discord-signed installer whose validation actually *failed* is invisible to a boolean and obvious with the signer name in hand.
- **Mismatch is the feature an adversary cannot cheaply satisfy.** Certificate theft is opportunistic: operators sign with whatever they obtained, not with a certificate whose owner plausibly ships the kind of software they are deploying. That forces a visible inconsistency between signer and artefact — a hardware vendor signing a TLS proxy, a chat company signing a document-sharing installer — and inconsistency is far more robust than rarity, which legitimate niche software also exhibits.
- **It composes with the other trust-surface work rather than duplicating it.** [[B048]] registers which certificate authorities a machine trusts, [[M045]] ranks kernel driver loads where signing is also the abused control, and this model registers which signing identities actually execute. Together they answer the three different questions that "is it signed?" collapses into one, and each produces a durable register rather than a stream of alerts.
