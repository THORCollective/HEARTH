hypothesis: >-
  When an agentic framework finds a registration endpoint it can reach without authentication, the account
  it mints is indistinguishable from a real signup at the moment of creation. What separates them is what
  the account does next. A genuine new user explores: a handful of pages, their own record, a slow start
  over days. An agent-driven account begins retrieving records immediately, enumerates identifiers it was
  never shown, and reads a volume that belongs to a reporting job rather than to a person. No static
  threshold generalises here, because the normal first-session footprint differs per application and per
  user class, so the hunt scores each new account against the distribution of genuine first sessions for
  that same application and ranks the outliers.

false_positive_notes: |
  Self-registration exists so that strangers can use the application, so the benign population is large and the model has to be fitted per application rather than per estate.
  - Integration and partner onboarding looks exactly like this. A customer wiring up a data sync registers an account and immediately pulls a large slice of the catalogue. These are the highest-scoring benign accounts in most environments. Maintain the list of accounts that are expected to behave like an integration, and keep it as data the scoring consumes, not as a note in a runbook.
  - Load tests, uptime monitors, SEO crawlers and accessibility scanners register accounts in staging and sometimes in production. Their signature is regularity across days rather than a single dense session; include session count and inter-session variance in the features so a monitor separates itself from a one-shot sweep.
  - Legitimate bulk export is a product feature in many applications. If a new account can click one button and receive ten thousand records, its first session is large by design and the score means nothing without the request path. Weight reads that walked sequential or guessable identifiers more heavily than reads served by a documented export endpoint.
  - Marketing campaigns and conference signups create registration spikes. A sudden cohort of new accounts is not itself the finding; the finding is a member of that cohort whose read behaviour is an outlier within it. Score against the cohort, not against the quiet weeks before it.
  - Response size is a poor proxy for record count on applications that paginate aggressively or compress. Where available, prefer the application's own count of records returned, and treat size-derived counts as a ranking input rather than as the reported number.
  - A single scored account proves very little. The combination that matters is a high first-session read score plus either an authorization decision that should have been a refusal or a subsequent privilege change, which is why both are inputs rather than follow-ups.
notes: |
  Source reporting is Unit 42's November 12, 2026 analysis of CL-STA-1238, in which Chinese-speaking attackers paired the open-source Cairn framework with the Zhipu GLM and DeepSeek models and ran 25 autonomous operations against at least 19 organizations between August and September 2026. Unit 42 confirmed unauthorized access or data retrieval at 10 of them. Two of the confirmed cases are this hunt exactly. At a U.S. teachers' union, "an unauthenticated account-creation bypass on the content management system allowed attackers to mint valid sessions and harvest member data at scale," and "the framework logged access to more than one million member records, roughly 31,000 CRM contacts and staff password hashes." At a defense-policy program, "attackers targeted an open backend registration endpoint on the identity platform supporting a defense-policy program. This exposed roughly 500 user records, including accounts using .mil and .gov domains."
  Why this is an Alchemy hunt rather than a Flames one - There is no artefact to match on. The account is valid, the session is valid, and every individual request is a request the application chose to answer. The only separator is magnitude and pace relative to what a new account normally does in that application, and that reference distribution is different for a members' CMS, a partner portal and an identity platform. A fixed number of records per session would be either blind or unusable depending on which application it was pointed at, which is the Alchemy trigger: a behaviour that varies enough by environment that a static threshold will not generalise.
  Closest existing HEARTH coverage - [[B069]] baselines the HTTP client population reaching internet-facing applications, which is the network-layer companion to this hunt and the right thing to run alongside it; it scores clients, not accounts, and it has no view of what records a session read. [[H450]] covers a web application service account spawning netcat or curl after an anonymous upload, which is the server-side execution path rather than the data-retrieval path. [[M067]] and [[M048]] score agent tempo on hosts and on security-product discovery. Nothing in the corpus scores an application account's first session, and nothing covers registration-endpoint abuse as an access path.
  Feature set - Per new account, over its first session and its first 24 hours: count of record-retrieval responses; count of distinct object identifiers returned; whether the identifier sequence is monotonic or dense within a numeric range; ratio of successful reads to authorization failures; count of distinct collections or endpoints touched; session duration and median inter-request interval; whether any read returned a record belonging to another principal; registration-to-first-read latency; the registering address's ASN and whether it is a hosting provider; the email domain's disposability; and whether the registration request itself was unauthenticated against an endpoint intended to require authentication.
  Scoring approach - Fit per application and per user class. For each feature, compute the percentile of the new account against the reference population of genuine first sessions, then combine. A robust approach that survives small populations is a per-feature median-absolute-deviation z-score summed into a single figure, which avoids assuming the first-session distribution is normal; it is heavily right-skewed in every application the author has measured. Rank descending and work the top of the list. The two features that should carry the most weight are distinct-object count and the read-to-refusal ratio: an account that walked identifiers and was refused almost nothing is either entitled to everything or found a broken authorization check.
  Core signal 1, the registration itself - Before scoring reads, establish which registration endpoints accept an unauthenticated request at all. Unit 42 lists the weaknesses used across the campaign: "Authentication bypasses", "Insecure direct object references", "Mass-assignment privilege escalation", "Weak and default credentials", "Exposed cloud APIs" and "Dated public CVEs". An account created by a request that reached a backend registration path without a session is a finding on its own, independent of what it later read, and it should be surfaced even when the read score is low.
  Core signal 2, the escalation that follows - At a semiconductor company Unit 42 reports the attackers "created unauthorized administrator accounts and a persistent API token in the production system" after brute-forcing a production credential. Join each scored account to administrative-change events in the same application for the following days: a role grant, a permission change, or an API token minted by or for a recently self-registered account is the sequence that turns a data-exposure finding into a persistence finding.
  Core signal 3, pace as corroboration, not as the detection - Unit 42 reports that "individual operations ran from 20 minutes to more than 50 hours with no attacker-imposed time limit," so a single tight burst is not guaranteed; some of this activity is slow. Use inter-request interval as a feature that raises a score, never as a filter that excludes an account. An operation that paced itself over two days still has an anomalous distinct-object count.
  Expected, not observed by Unit 42 - Any specific per-session record threshold, and the request paths or parameter names used against the affected applications. Unit 42 reports the outcomes and the weakness classes but does not publish the requests, so the identifier-walking feature above is a generalisation from the IDOR and mass-assignment classes it names rather than something it observed request by request.
  Infrastructure, for retrospective scoping only - Unit 42 attributes the campaign to a customized Cairn fork run from 154.44.31[.]4 in Hong Kong with an egress and staging node at 82.29.129[.]54 in the U.S. Use these to bound a look-back over registration logs; the scoring is the detection.
  Scope - Every internet-facing application that permits self-registration, and in particular content management systems holding member or constituent data, partner and customer portals, and identity platforms fronting a programme rather than an estate. Unit 42's target set - semiconductor supply chain, defense policy, human rights and civil society - is a reminder that the applications holding the most sensitive membership data are frequently the ones administered with the fewest people.

# M071

The account was real. That is the problem.

Unit 42 traced 25 autonomous operations run through the Cairn framework against 19 organizations over about two weeks, driven by Chinese commercial models and steered by nothing more than a one-line goal. Ten organizations lost data. At a teachers' union, the path in was an unauthenticated account-creation bypass on the content management system: the framework minted valid sessions and then read more than a million member records, around 31,000 CRM contacts, and staff password hashes. At a defense-policy programme, an open backend registration endpoint on the identity platform gave up roughly 500 user records, including `.mil` and `.gov` accounts.

There is no malware in that story. No implant, no C2 beacon on the application host, no process to notice. A stranger signed up, which the application was built to allow, and then asked for records, which the application answered.

So the only thing left to measure is proportion. A person who registers for a members' site reads their own record and maybe a dozen pages. An agent reads the collection. That difference is enormous — and it is also completely specific to the application, which is why it has to be scored rather than thresholded. Ten thousand records in a first session is an outrage on a union membership site and a Tuesday on a partner data feed.

Two features carry most of the signal. How many distinct records did this brand-new account actually get back, and how often was it told no? An account that walked a range of identifiers and was refused almost nothing has either been given the keys deliberately, or has found an authorization check that does not work.

## Why

- **Nothing about the access is anomalous except its size.** The credential is legitimate, the session is legitimate, and each request is one the application chose to serve. Signature and artefact hunting have nothing to key on, which leaves magnitude relative to a reference population as the only available discriminator.
- **Autonomy is what makes the volume reachable.** Unit 42's closing point is that "autonomous orchestration removes the historical limit on the number of targets an actor could pursue simultaneously due to operator hours." The same removal applies within a target: nobody paginates through a million records by hand, so the volume itself is new even though the weakness is not.
- **It scores the access path most organizations do not inventory.** Self-registration endpoints are a product decision, often made years earlier, and they rarely appear in an attack-surface review next to the VPN and the mail gateway. Running this hunt forces the list to exist, and the list is useful whether or not the scoring finds anything.
- **The weaknesses were all ordinary.** Authentication bypasses, IDOR, mass assignment, weak credentials, exposed cloud APIs, dated CVEs. Unit 42's own conclusion is that "autonomous AI raises the volume and coverage of exploitation, but not its complexity" — so this is a hunt that pays off against the unglamorous half of the application estate, not against novel exploitation.
- **The escalation join turns exposure into persistence.** At the semiconductor target the framework created administrator accounts and a persistent API token after it got in. A scored account that later receives a role or mints a token is no longer a data-exposure question; joining those two event streams is cheap and changes the severity of the finding entirely.
