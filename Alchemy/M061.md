  `CloudAppEvents | where Timestamp > ago(30d) | where Application in~ ("Microsoft SharePoint Online", "Microsoft OneDrive for Business") | extend Site = tostring(RawEventData.SiteUrl), Op = ActionType, Anon = tostring(RawEventData.EventSource) | summarize AnonLinksCreated = countif(Op == "AnonymousLinkCreated"), AnonLinkUses = countif(Op == "AnonymousLinkUsed"), InternalReads = countif(Op in ("FileAccessed", "FileDownloaded") and isnotempty(AccountObjectId)), SourceNetworks = dcount(IPAddress), Files = dcount(tostring(RawEventData.ObjectId)) by Site | extend ExternalRatio = todouble(AnonLinkUses) / todouble(InternalReads + 1) | where AnonLinkUses > 0 | order by ExternalRatio desc`

  stack of thresholds. It percentile-ranks each feature within the tenant and sorts by the product, so

  `import csv, sys`
  `rows = list(csv.DictReader(sys.stdin))`
  `features = ["AnonLinksCreated", "ExternalRatio", "SourceNetworks", "OrphanFiles"]`
  `n = len(rows)`
  `for feat in features:`
  `    order = sorted(rows, key=lambda r: float(r.get(feat) or 0))`
  `    for rank, row in enumerate(order, start=1):`
  `        row[feat + "_pct"] = rank / n`
  `for row in rows:`
  `    score = 1.0`
  `    for feat in features:`
  `        score *= row[feat + "_pct"]`
  `    row["score"] = score`
  `for row in sorted(rows, key=lambda r: -r["score"])[:20]:`

# M061

Nothing was stolen. That is what makes this hard.

Microsoft describes what Storm-3119 does with a hijacked Microsoft 365 tenant once it holds SharePoint control: the actor "abuses the compromised tenant's SharePoint and OneDrive as bulk hosting and converts the hijacked storage into a sellable product."

Read that as a defender and notice what is absent. No data leaves the tenant. No unusual download volume by an employee. No DLP policy triggers, because nothing sensitive is moving. The storage bill goes up slightly. Files appear and are served to people outside the organisation, and that is the entire incident.

The asset being monetised is not the gigabytes. It is the domain.

A phishing link that resolves to your SharePoint tenant inherits your reputation — with mail filters, with URL reputation services, with a user who hovers over the link and sees a company they have heard of. Microsoft notes the marketplace "offers a variety of infrastructure based on age, subscription type, and environment types such as education (.edu) or non-profit (.org)", and uses automation to find "expired environment or organization names" whose "legitimate customer information" helps "circumvent existing detections." Provenance is the product. Your tenant's good name is being rented out to attack other people.

So what is there to detect?

Direction. A site whose reads come overwhelmingly from outside, through anonymous links, from many unrelated networks, for files that nobody inside the organisation has ever opened.

And here is the problem with turning that into a rule: it is also a precise description of a successful marketing campaign.

Anonymous links are a supported feature. Heavy external downloads are the *purpose* of external sharing. Access from hundreds of networks is what popular content looks like. There is no threshold on any one of those signals that separates a hijacked tenant from a company that published a whitepaper people actually wanted. Pick one and you will either flag every public site or miss the hosting entirely.

Which is why this is a ranking, not an alert. Percentile-rank each site within the tenant on anonymous-link creation rate against its own history, external-to-internal read ratio, source-network diversity, and the share of accessed files with no internal activity. Multiply the ranks. A site has to be strange on several axes at once to reach the top.

Two things make or break it in practice.

The first is the site-to-audience inventory, which is listed as a required data source rather than a nice-to-have. Without a label for "this site is supposed to be public", your genuinely public sites will own the top of the list and the analytic gets switched off inside a week.

The second is the feature that holds up best against all the legitimate explanations: the absence of an internal lifecycle. A real public site is still edited, reviewed and opened by insiders — someone owns it, someone updates it, someone reads it. Third-party hosting serves outsiders only. Content that strangers consume and employees have never touched is a narrower thing than content that is merely popular.

One caveat to state plainly, because it affects how much the scoring can claim. Microsoft reports the storage abuse but does not describe the sharing mechanics, the file types, the content or any volumes. The features above are the ordinary observable consequences of hosting third-party content on this platform — they are inference about mechanism, not reported detail. The report does not say the actor used anonymous links.

## Why

- **Nothing exfiltrates, so loss-oriented detection is silent.** DLP, download-volume alerting and insider-risk scoring are all keyed on data leaving, and here the victim's storage is filling up rather than draining.
- **The domain's reputation is the asset.** Hosted phishing content inherits the tenant's standing with mail filters and URL reputation services, which is why a hijacked tenant is worth more than its quota.
- **Every individual signal is normal.** Anonymous links, external reads and wide network diversity are each ordinary, so only their joint rarity and deviation from a site's own history carries information.
- **A per-site baseline beats a tenant-wide one.** A quiet internal site that suddenly starts sharing is the finding; the same absolute numbers on a marketing site are a good quarter.
- **Absence of internal lifecycle is the durable discriminator.** Legitimate public content is still edited and opened by insiders, while resold hosting serves outsiders exclusively — a distinction that survives contact with real tenants.
- **The enabling grant is queryable and nearby.** SharePoint full control and app catalog write appear in the Entra audit log days before the sharing, which makes a scored outlier confirmable rather than merely suspicious.
- **The victim is the unwitting host, which changes the response.** Remediation means revoking the application and the links and notifying the abused brand's targets, not restoring files or chasing a data loss.
- **Ranking keeps the analytic alive.** A ranked top-twenty reviewed against an owner inventory survives in an operational queue, where a threshold rule on anonymous sharing would be muted within days.
