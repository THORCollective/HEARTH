# M037

Unit 42's framing of this problem is the reason it belongs in Alchemy rather than Flames:
"Authentication alone is no longer sufficient to establish trust." Every event in this attack chain is
an authenticated user performing a permitted action. External federation is working exactly as designed.
There is no malformed request, no anomalous protocol, no unsigned binary — the adversary sends a chat
message, which is what the platform is for.

The report is also explicit about where detection cannot happen: "Security teams might not detect the
initial collaboration message directly." Its own published query — Cortex XQL matching Slack or Teams as
the immediate parent or ancestor of a shell — deliberately lands downstream, at the execution stage. That
is a sound detection and worth having, but it only fires after the social engineering has already
succeeded and something has run. The gap is the first-contact event itself.

That gap is a scoring problem. A single external one-on-one chat from an unfamiliar tenant is
unremarkable and happens legitimately hundreds of times a month in any organization that works with
partners. The same tenant opening first contact with fourteen employees across five unrelated
departments in twenty minutes, from a `*.onmicrosoft.com` domain registered last week, with a display
name like "Corporate IT Service (Internal)" — that is a different object entirely, and it is only
visible when you aggregate by tenant rather than evaluate by message.

The scale argument supports treating this as its own surface: collaboration-tool endpoint alerts more
than quadrupled over roughly a year, and 99% of the resulting alerts related to chat phishing
operations. Unlike email, these platforms let an attacker respond in real time and adjust their pretext
mid-conversation, so the pressure on the target is qualitatively higher than a static phish.

## Why

- **No static threshold generalizes, which is the defining Alchemy trigger.** The fan-out that indicates
  an attack in a 200-person company is unremarkable in a 50,000-person company with an active partner
  ecosystem. The right cutoff varies by tenant size, industry, partner density and season. A model that
  scores relative to each organization's own observed distribution of external first contact is the only
  form of this detection that transfers between environments.
- **The signal exists only in aggregate.** Per-message evaluation is structurally incapable of seeing
  this: each individual `ChatCreated` event is legitimate and low-information. Novelty, fan-out, burst
  rate and recipient dissimilarity are all properties of the *tenant-to-organization edge* over a time
  window, not of any single event. This is a graph and volumetric problem by construction.
- **Ranking beats alerting for a behavior this common.** External chats are far too voluminous for
  one-alert-per-event to be operationally viable — an alerting rule here gets muted. A daily ranked list
  of the top external tenants by anomaly score gives a hunter a bounded, prioritized queue and degrades
  gracefully: on a quiet day the top of the list is benign onboarding, which is a useful negative result
  rather than a false positive.
- **Downstream engagement is a strong, cheap feature that converts the score into a verdict.** Microsoft's
  cross-tenant helpdesk impersonation playbook describes precisely this correlation chain — a
  `ChatCreated` first contact followed by vishing, remote management, and mailbombing or URL-click
  alerts, all joinable on account and chat thread. Adding "did any recipient subsequently accept, receive
  a file, or launch Quick Assist / AnyDesk / TeamViewer" as a weighted feature sharpens the ranking
  dramatically without requiring a separate detection. Order the join so the chat precedes the execution.
- **Impersonated MOERA domains are a durable, learnable feature rather than an IOC.** Most malicious
  activity in this pattern arrives from impersonated `*.onmicrosoft.com` tenants with
  authority-suggesting display names. Individual domains rotate constantly and are worthless as
  indicators, but "is a MOERA domain," "domain age," and "display name asserts an internal IT or support
  role" are stable features that survive rotation — exactly the kind of input a model can use and a
  blocklist cannot.
- **The identity controls organizations rely on do not address this.** As Unit 42 notes, MFA and
  conditional access do not prevent an attacker from misusing a valid session, and a compromised account
  inherits its owner's identity context — permissions, relationships and ongoing conversations. When the
  initiating account is legitimately compromised rather than attacker-registered, tenant-reputation
  features fail and the behavioral fan-out signal is what remains.
- **HEARTH had no T1684.001 coverage at all**, despite collaboration-platform impersonation now being one
  of the most active initial-access vectors and one that email-centric phishing controls do not see.

## Notes — modeling approach

**UNIT OF ANALYSIS: the external tenant (OrganizationId), per rolling window** — not the individual chat,
and not the recipient. Aggregate all `ChatCreated` events attributable to a foreign tenant within a
sliding window (start with 24 hours, and separately 7 days for slow-burn campaigns).

**CANDIDATE FEATURES:**

- *Tenant novelty* — first-ever appearance of this OrganizationId; days since first observation; whether
  it appears on the federated-partner allowlist.
- *Fan-out* — distinct internal recipients contacted in the window, normalized against the organization's
  own historical per-tenant distribution rather than an absolute number.
- *Burst rate* — recipients contacted per unit time; automated outreach is temporally tighter than
  organic collaboration.
- *Recipient dissimilarity* — spread of recipients across departments, roles, locations and managers. A
  real vendor contacts a project team; an attacker contacts whoever they harvested. Consultants are the
  known confounder here.
- *Domain features* — MOERA (`*.onmicrosoft.com`) status, domain registration age, display-name tokens
  asserting IT/support/helpdesk authority, and geo of the sender IP from `MessageSent`.
- *Acceptance rate* — proportion of recipients generating `UserAccepted`. Useful as a positive signal;
  do not treat its absence as evidence of non-engagement given the observed inconsistency.
- *Downstream engagement* — any recipient executing an RMM/remote-assistance binary, or any shell
  spawned with Teams as an ancestor, within a short window AFTER the chat. Weight this heavily; it moves
  a tenant from "anomalous" to "investigate now."
- *Reciprocity* — whether internal users initiate chats back to this tenant over time. Genuine
  partnerships are bidirectional and long-lived; campaigns are one-directional and brief.

**MODEL SELECTION:** start with a transparent weighted score over normalized features, not a black-box
model. The feature set is small, the classes are extremely imbalanced, and labeled positives are scarce —
conditions under which a tuned linear score is more defensible and far easier for an analyst to act on
than an unsupervised ensemble. Graduate to isolation-forest or similar density-based scoring only once
you have enough labeled outcomes to validate it, and keep per-feature contributions visible in the output
either way. An analyst must be able to read *why* a tenant ranked highly.

**VALIDATION:** backtest against known partner-onboarding events, which should score high on novelty and
fan-out but low on dissimilarity, domain-age and downstream-engagement features — if onboarding is
indistinguishable from a campaign in your scoring, the feature weights need work before the model goes
live. Then confirm the pipeline fires end to end by generating a benign external chat from a test tenant.

**OUTPUT:** a daily ranked list of external tenants with per-feature contributions, the recipient list,
and any downstream engagement — reviewed as a hunting queue, not routed as individual alerts.

**WHY THIS IS ALCHEMY AND NOT A FLAME OR AN EMBER.** Several Alchemy triggers apply. The behavior is
detectable by anomaly rather than signature — every constituent event is legitimate and authenticated.
Rule-based detection would be far too noisy to run at scale in any organization with real partner
traffic. Per-organization thresholds are mandatory because fan-out norms vary enormously with company
size and industry. And the analysis is fundamentally graph-shaped: an edge between an external tenant and
a set of internal identities, scored on the shape of that edge. **Flames was rejected** because there is
no discrete observable to hypothesize about — "an external user sent a chat message" is not a huntable
behavior, and the only crisp behavioral artifact in the chain (Teams parenting a shell) is a downstream
execution event that fires after compromise, better served as its own narrow rule than as the primary
detection. **Embers was a genuine contender and is partially subsumed here:** a baseline of legitimate
federated partner tenants is real, necessary work, and it is captured above as the allowlist that must be
built before scoring. But that allowlist alone does not produce detection — every new legitimate partner
is by definition off-baseline — so the baseline is an input to the model rather than the deliverable, and
the scoring layer is what turns it into something actionable.
