false_positive_notes: |
  Driver loads are high-volume and overwhelmingly legitimate, so this must be built as a ranking rather than as an alert, and the benign populations need to be understood before the first review. The bulk of loads are Microsoft in-box drivers from `%SystemRoot%\System32\drivers\`, which are ubiquitous and should sit at the bottom of any rarity score automatically. Above them sit enterprise-deployed third-party drivers — EDR and antivirus, VPN clients, disk encryption, virtualisation, printer and scanner drivers, and hardware management agents — and these are the population that most resembles the target in that they are third-party and kernel-mode. They are distinguished by shape rather than by identity: they land on a coherent, management-channel-defined population of hundreds or thousands of hosts, they install into standard driver paths, they load persistently across reboots, and their deployment correlates with a software-distribution record. Expect a genuine long tail that is benign and will recur at the top of the ranking until dispositioned: engineering and laboratory machines with unusual hardware, a developer testing a signed driver, legacy peripherals with old drivers, and remote-access or hardware-diagnostic tools run by IT for one-off troubleshooting. That last category is the most persistent source of benign top-ranked hits, because a technician running a vendor diagnostic utility produces exactly the single-host, load-once, unusual-path profile the model is built to surface — resolve these by correlating with the service desk rather than by widening the suppression list. Old signing certificates deserve particular care: certificate age is a useful weight because many known-abusable drivers are old, but plenty of legitimate hardware ships with long-lived drivers, so age must never contribute more than a modest weight. Finally, the absence of a CodeIntegrity block is not exoneration — the Microsoft blocklist is not exhaustive and is precisely what an actor selects around when choosing a driver.
notes: |
  Model shape - a per-driver-load score combining four weighted inputs, surfaced as a ranked daily list. (1) FLEET PREVALENCE - the number of distinct hosts that have loaded this driver hash over 90 days, scored inversely; a hash on one host out of forty thousand is the top of the list. (2) PATH LEGITIMACY - whether `ImageLoaded` resolves inside `%SystemRoot%\System32\drivers\` or somewhere else; `%TEMP%`, `%APPDATA%`, `%PROGRAMDATA%` and user-profile paths score heavily. (3) SIGNING-CERTIFICATE AGE - the gap between the signing certificate''s validity period and now, contributing a modest weight. (4) LOAD PERSISTENCE - whether the driver loads at every boot (legitimate installed software) or appeared exactly once (the BYOVD profile). Add a decisive multiplier for any hash matching the LOLDrivers reference set, and a second for any CodeIntegrity block event on the same host.
  Reported artefact from the source chain - the ClearFake/Amatera chain terminated EDR processes by identifying them via name HASHING and then sending IOCTL `0x2205c0` to a vulnerable driver, `DCRCVDrv.sys`, which invoked `ZwTerminateProcess` from kernel mode. Retain the driver name and IOCTL as pivots, but do not build the detection on them - the whole point of BYOVD is that the specific driver is interchangeable, and there are hundreds of candidates.
  Correlating signal that raises a hit to an incident - a rare driver load followed within minutes by security-tooling processes terminating unexpectedly, by an EDR agent going silent, or by a gap in expected telemetry from that host. Because the kill happens from kernel mode, the endpoint agent may produce no record of its own death, so hunt for the SILENCE - a host that stops reporting while remaining reachable on the network is the strongest possible corroboration and is often the only evidence the agent could ever have produced.
  Companion query worth running alongside - Sysmon Event ID 11 for `.sys` files created anywhere outside `%SystemRoot%\System32\drivers\`, and System event 7045 registering a kernel-mode service whose `ImagePath` points to such a location. A driver written to `%TEMP%` and registered as a service is close to self-evidently worth reviewing regardless of what the prevalence model says, and gives a cheap standing detection while the model is being tuned.
  Preventive control to check as an output of this hunt - Microsoft''s vulnerable driver blocklist (enabled by default with HVCI/Memory Integrity, and separately configurable) blocks a curated set of known-abusable drivers. Report, as a deliverable of this hunt, what proportion of the estate has HVCI and the blocklist enabled - that number is usually much lower than assumed, and raising it removes a large share of the BYOVD candidate space outright.
  Output shape and triage - a ranked daily list with, per entry: driver hash, `ImageLoaded` path, signer name, certificate validity dates, host count over 90 days, first-seen timestamp, loading process, whether the hash is in LOLDrivers, and whether any CodeIntegrity or EDR-silence event followed on the same host. Disposition each recurring benign entry once into an allowlist keyed on hash plus expected host population, so the list shortens over time rather than repeating.
  Why this is Alchemy rather than Flames - the malicious driver is validly signed and loaded through a documented mechanism, so there is no signature, path or command line that generalises. Known-bad lists catch only what has already been enumerated, and BYOVD specifically selects around them. The only durable discriminator is how the driver''s deployment population compares to that of legitimate drivers in the same fleet, which is a relative, per-environment measure.

# M045

Cisco Talos's ClearFake analysis includes a step that is now routine in commodity intrusions: before stealing anything, the loader disabled endpoint protection by loading a vulnerable signed driver, `DCRCVDrv.sys`, identifying security processes by hashing their names, and sending IOCTL `0x2205c0` to have the driver call `ZwTerminateProcess` from kernel mode. Bring-your-own-vulnerable-driver works because it inverts the trust model rather than defeating it. The driver is genuinely signed by a real vendor, so signature validation confirms it. Windows loads it through the ordinary service mechanism, so nothing is bypassed. The IOCTL is a legitimate device-control operation. Blocklists catch only the drivers already enumerated, and the candidate pool runs to hundreds, so an actor simply picks one that is not on the list. What does not generalise for the adversary is deployment shape: legitimate drivers arrive on coherent populations through management channels, and BYOVD drivers appear once, on one host, from a path no installer would use.

## Why

- **This technique is specifically designed to survive every static check, so a relative model is not a stylistic preference but the only option.** Signed, documented load mechanism, legitimate IOCTL, valid vendor — each individual property is indistinguishable from legitimate software, and each is verified as good by the controls examining it. Only the comparison against how drivers normally arrive in *this* fleet separates them.
- **Blocklists are structurally one step behind and the adversary chooses accordingly.** The Microsoft vulnerable driver blocklist and LOLDrivers are valuable and should be used as strong weights, but they enumerate what is already known while the abusable population is large and growing. Prevalence scoring catches the driver nobody has catalogued yet, which is precisely the one an actor will select.
- **Deployment population is a discriminator the adversary cannot easily fake.** To make a BYOVD driver look legitimate by this measure, an actor would need it present across a large, coherent, management-channel-defined slice of the estate — which is the very thing a targeted intrusion cannot arrange. That gives the model a durable edge rather than a temporary one.
- **The consequence of a successful load is telemetry loss, so the corroborating signal is an absence.** When the driver kills the EDR agent from kernel mode, the agent may never record its own termination. Pairing rare driver loads with hosts that go quiet while remaining network-reachable catches the outcome even when the endpoint product could not report it — and that pairing is only possible if someone is explicitly watching for silence.
- **Running it produces a control-coverage number the organisation probably does not have.** HVCI/Memory Integrity and the driver blocklist remove much of the BYOVD candidate space, and their real deployment rate across an estate is almost always lower than assumed. Measuring that coverage is a concrete, durable output of the hunt independent of whether any malicious driver is found.
