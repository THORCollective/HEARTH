false_positive_notes: |
  Daily-cadence traffic is overwhelmingly legitimate. Update checks, certificate revocation refreshes, licence validation, telemetry uploads, backup schedules and scheduled reporting all produce one or a few connections a day with low variance. This hunt is not usable without a maintained allowlist of those endpoints; scored raw, the top of the ranking will be entirely update infrastructure.
  Scheduled tasks and cron-driven business jobs produce extremely regular daily connections - often more regular than an implant with jitter. Low variance alone therefore ranks legitimate automation above the target. The jitter band matters: the report describes a 22-to-28-hour spread, so a destination contacted at exactly the same second every day is more likely a scheduler than this implant.
  A host that is powered off overnight, or a laptop that roams, breaks the inter-arrival series and will produce apparent regime changes that are artifacts of availability rather than tasking. Normalise against host uptime before scoring, and discard series with large unexplained gaps rather than treating the gap edges as transitions.
  The regime-switch signal has a genuine benign analogue: software that polls slowly when idle and rapidly during an active download or an interactive session behaves exactly this way. Package managers, update agents and conferencing clients all do it. Process attribution is what separates them, which is why this hunt is weak without it.
  A clean result means much less than it appears to if retention is shorter than roughly 60 days. With a 24-hour period, a 30-day window yields on the order of 26 samples and a 7-day window yields 7, which cannot support a variance estimate. Record the retention actually available alongside any negative finding.
notes: |
  Platform - Windows, for the host-side features; the cadence analysis itself runs on network records and is platform-independent. This is the command-and-control stage of the chain covered by the two paired hunts below.
  Why this is a model-assisted hunt and not a rule - there is no indicator to match and no threshold that generalises. The implant's defining property is an absence: too few connections to be interesting, spread too widely to be periodic within a normal window. The usable signals are statistical and relative - how rare this destination is for this host, how tight the daily spread is, and whether the cadence has changed regime - and all three have to be scored together because each on its own is dominated by legitimate automation.
  What the report states, verbatim - on the dormant interval: the loop "pauses for 22-28 hours, checking every 10 minutes to determine whether the allotted time has elapsed". On the active interval: "If a valid response is received from C2, the loop pauses for 5-6 minutes before the next request". On the pre-beacon delay: the implant waits "a random jitter delay of 20-40 minutes" before the initial request "to evade sandboxes by causing them to time out". Kaspersky summarises the design intent directly: "This item indicates that the malware uses very long polling intervals." The report also records a watchdog timer of approximately 28 hours and a C2 server list with a current-index field, which is why a fallback-channel technique is tagged.
  The feature set to score - compute per (host, destination) pair over at least 60 days:
  1. Sample count. The target sits in a narrow band: roughly 20 to 70 connections over 60 days. Fewer is noise; far more is ordinary traffic.
  2. Median inter-arrival time, with a target band of 22 to 28 hours. Score the median rather than the mean, because a single long outage skews the mean badly at this timescale.
  3. Dispersion of inter-arrival times, as the median absolute deviation divided by the median. The report's 22-to-28-hour spread implies a ratio in the low tens of percent - distinctly looser than a scheduled task and distinctly tighter than human browsing.
  4. Destination rarity across the fleet. A daily beacon to a destination that one host contacts and nothing else does is far more interesting than a daily beacon to a destination every host contacts.
  5. Request-size stability, as the coefficient of variation of bytes sent. A constant-size encrypted check-in is a corroborator.
  6. Regime change, which is the highest-value feature and is described below.
  The regime switch is the alertable event - a dormant implant is nearly invisible and a tasked one is not. The transition from a 22-to-28-hour cadence to a 5-to-6-minute cadence is a change of three orders of magnitude, it happens to one destination on one host, and it means an operator is interacting. Detect it by segmenting each series and comparing the median inter-arrival time either side of a candidate breakpoint; alert when the ratio exceeds roughly two orders of magnitude and the post-change segment holds at least a handful of samples. The practical value of ranking the dormant state at all is that it gives you the candidate list to watch for this transition, so the scarcity score and the change detector are two halves of one hunt rather than alternatives.
  Worked arithmetic, computed rather than recalled - the dormant band the report gives as 79,200 to 100,800 seconds is 22.0 to 28.0 hours, so over a 60-day window a dormant implant produces between 51 and 65 connections, and over a 30-day window between 25 and 32. The active band of 5 to 6 minutes produces between 240 and 288 connections a day. The ratio of dormant to active cadence is therefore between roughly 220:1 and 336:1, which is the margin the change detector is working with and the reason a two-order-of-magnitude threshold is comfortable rather than marginal.
  Why the Defender XDR window is the wrong instrument here, and what to use instead - advanced hunting retains 30 days, which yields at most about 32 samples of a 24-hour period and cannot support a dispersion estimate for a long series. Use Defender for the process attribution and the host-side pre-beacon delay, and run the cadence analysis against proxy, firewall or DNS records with longer retention. This query collects the attribution half within the available window:
  ```kusto
  DeviceNetworkEvents
  | where Timestamp > ago(30d)
  | where RemoteIPType == "Public"
  | summarize Connections = count(), BytesSentCv = stdev(toreal(coalesce(tolong(column_ifexists("SentBytes", 0)), 0))),
              FirstSeen = min(Timestamp), LastSeen = max(Timestamp),
              Processes = make_set(InitiatingProcessFileName, 8)
            by DeviceId, DeviceName, RemoteUrl
  | extend WindowHours = datetime_diff('hour', LastSeen, FirstSeen)
  | where Connections between (20 .. 70) and WindowHours > 240
  | extend MeanIntervalHours = todouble(WindowHours) / todouble(Connections - 1)
  | where MeanIntervalHours between (18.0 .. 32.0)
  | project DeviceName, RemoteUrl, Connections, WindowHours, MeanIntervalHours, Processes
  | order by MeanIntervalHours asc
  ```
  This is a candidate generator, not a detection: the mean interval it computes is a crude stand-in for the median, the band is deliberately wider than the report's to absorb that, and every result needs the allowlist applied before a human looks at it. Treat a hit as a destination to pull the full-retention series for.
  The host-side corroborator - the report describes a 20-to-40-minute wait before the first request. That gives a second, independent feature that does not need long retention: the delay between a process starting and its first outbound connection. A process that starts, sits for half an hour, then makes one connection and goes quiet is unusual on its own terms, and it is measurable inside a 30-day window. It is also the feature most likely to be absent in practice, because the implant runs inside a COM surrogate that may have started long before the retention window opens.
  Inference, not stated by Kaspersky - every feature, threshold, band and query above is mine, including the two-order-of-magnitude change threshold, the 20-to-70 sample band, the dispersion ratio and the 60-day retention requirement. Kaspersky published the implant's configured intervals, not a detection method, and did not publish cadence observations from a victim network. The arithmetic in the worked example is computed from the report's own second values. Kaspersky names one C2 domain in the analysis; it is deliberately not reproduced here as a hunting input, because the whole point of this hunt is that it does not depend on knowing the destination.
  Pairing - [[H437]] covers the loader stage of this chain and [[H438]] covers the stage that executes in a COM surrogate and owns the network session this hunt scores. A hit here should be triaged against both, because the process attribution that makes this hunt usable will point at the surrogate H438 describes.
  Closest existing coverage - [[M065]] is the nearest existing hunt and is the deliberate opposite tail of the same problem: it scores an implant polling roughly four times a second, where the extreme request rate and near-zero variance are the features. This hunt scores the other end, where the period exceeds the hunting window and scarcity is the feature. The two share a framing - that interval-and-jitter models are tuned for a middle band and blind outside it - but they need different retention, different feature sets and different thresholds, and neither would surface the other's target. [[M002]] applies a model to beaconing in encrypted DNS traffic, which is a different transport and does not address the long-period case. [[M006]] is unrelated here, covering dictionary-based domain generation rather than cadence.

# M066

Beacon hunting has a comfortable band. Implants that check in every few minutes with some jitter produce enough samples inside a week of logs to fit an interval, and the regularity is the tell. NetamLoader sits outside that band on purpose, and the way it gets there is almost trivial: it makes its period longer than the window anyone is looking at.

Kaspersky's analysis gives the numbers. When the C2 does not answer, the loop "pauses for 22-28 hours, checking every 10 minutes to determine whether the allotted time has elapsed." Before the very first request the implant waits "a random jitter delay of 20-40 minutes" specifically "to evade sandboxes by causing them to time out." The report's own summary is blunt: "This item indicates that the malware uses very long polling intervals."

At roughly one connection a day, a dormant implant is not a faint signal — it is fewer data points than a model needs. Over 30 days of retention it produces something like 25 to 32 connections, which cannot support a variance estimate, and what it does produce looks exactly like a licence check or an update poll. That is the real finding here, and it is a collection finding before it is an analytic one: a 24-hour period needs on the order of 60 days of records to be characterisable at all, so this hunt cannot be run inside a 30-day advanced-hunting window no matter how it is written.

What makes the implant catchable is that it has two speeds. Kaspersky records that "If a valid response is received from C2, the loop pauses for 5-6 minutes before the next request." Dormant, it is invisible; tasked, it is noisy. The cadence between those states differs by between roughly 220 and 336 times — computed from the report's own interval values — and a change of that size on one destination for one host is not something legitimate traffic does quietly.

So the hunt is two halves that only work together. Scoring scarcity — a destination contacted once a day, by one host, with a loose daily spread and a constant small request size — produces a candidate list that is mostly update infrastructure and needs an allowlist to be readable at all. Watching those candidates for a regime switch to minute-scale polling is what turns the list into a finding. Neither half stands alone: the scarcity score has too many false positives to alert on, and the change detector has nowhere to look without the candidate list.

## Why

- The implant's period is longer than the window most beacon analytics run over, so it is not detected poorly — it is not sampled enough to be detected at all, which is a different and more serious problem.
- The dormant state is behaviorally identical to benign daily automation, which means no threshold on cadence alone can separate them and the hunt has to lean on destination rarity and process attribution.
- The bimodal design creates a crisp, rare, high-contrast event at exactly the moment the implant matters, which is when an operator is interacting with it.
- The pre-beacon delay of 20 to 40 minutes is aimed at sandbox timeouts, so dynamic analysis of the sample is likely to have recorded no network activity whatsoever and will not have produced the destination for a blocklist.
- The implant validates its server certificate against its own configured value, so TLS inspection yields no content and cadence is most of what a network sensor can contribute.
- Stating the retention actually available is part of reporting this hunt honestly: below roughly 60 days a clean result is close to meaningless, and recording that is more useful than recording the clean result.
