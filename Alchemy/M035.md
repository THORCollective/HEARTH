hypothesis: >-
  Once `secretsdump.py` has pulled the NTDS hashes off a domain controller, the operator does not need a single
  password to move anywhere in the estate. Pass-the-hash produces authentication that is cryptographically valid
  and, event-for-event, identical to legitimate NTLM logons — the only high-fidelity artifact, a 4624 with
  LogonType 9 and logon process `seclogo`, appears only on the SOURCE host, which is the one host the operator
  controls and can stop logging. What they cannot suppress is the authentication graph on the destinations:
  account-to-host edges that have never existed before, appearing in a burst, from a source that has never
  spoken to those destinations, over NTLM in an estate that otherwise negotiates Kerberos. Score each
  (source, account, destination) edge on the joint rarity of novelty, fan-out, protocol choice, and burst
  timing against 90 days of the estate's own authentication history.

false_positive_notes: |
  NTLM is still everywhere, and authentication is the highest-volume telemetry in most estates. The naive
  "NTLM logon" query returns millions of events. Expect legitimate volume from:
  - Any connection made by IP address rather than by hostname/SPN — administrators, scripts, monitoring, and
    scanners all do this constantly, and it falls back to NTLM every time. This is the single largest benign
    population and it is enormous.
  - Legacy applications, appliances, NAS devices, printers and scan-to-folder, and non-Windows SMB clients that
    cannot do Kerberos at all.
  - Vulnerability scanners and configuration-management tools authenticating to broad host sets by design. These
    produce exactly the fan-out signature this hunt looks for, at a far larger scale than any operator.
  - Backup agents, inventory agents, and patch-management systems touching every host on a schedule.
  - Workgroup and non-domain-joined systems, and cross-forest scenarios without full trust.
  - Local account authentication, which is inherently NTLM.
  - Genuine administrative work during an incident or a maintenance window.
  This is why the hunt is scored rather than thresholded. The model must:
  (1) Baseline PER EDGE and PER ACCOUNT, not globally. A vulnerability scanner touching four thousand hosts every
      Tuesday has a stable, enormous, recognisable profile and should score near zero forever — fan-out is
      normal FOR IT. The signal is an account whose own history is narrow suddenly going wide.
  (2) Treat scanners, backup agents, and config-management identities as enumerated populations with their own
      baselines, never as global suppressions. A suppression on a service account is precisely the thing an
      operator wants to inherit; a per-identity baseline still catches that account doing something new.
  (3) Weight NOVELTY of the (source, account, destination) triple far above raw volume. The operator's edges are
      new; the scanner's are not.
  (4) Score the PROTOCOL CHOICE in context. NTLM is only meaningful where Kerberos was possible and not used.
      Establish, per destination, whether it can do Kerberos at all before treating NTLM as a signal — the
      appliance that has never done Kerberos in its life is not evidence.
  (5) Do not build the model on LogonType 9 / seclogo. It is genuinely high-fidelity, and it is source-side, so
      it exists only where the operator's own host is logging and forwarding. Score it as a strong confirming
      feature when present; never require it.
  Expect the first two to three weeks of scoring to be dominated by unmodelled scanner and agent identities.
  Enumerate and name them before treating any score as actionable, and re-baseline after infrastructure changes.
notes: |
  PLATFORM: Windows / Active Directory. Destination-side event collection across the member-server and
  workstation estate is the prerequisite; a DC-only collection posture will see 4776 but will miss most of the
  graph and cannot compute fan-out reliably.

  SOURCE: CISA / FBI joint advisory AA26-222A, "#StopRansomware: Gunra Ransomware". The relevant finding: "The
  FBI observed multiple instances of Gunra actors using secretsdump.py (another Impacket library) to conduct OS
  credential dumping [T1003.003] against compromised domain controllers to extract password hashes of user
  accounts from the NT Directory Services (NTDS) file. This enabled pass-the-hash [T1550.002] or pass-the-ticket
  [T1550.003] attacks for lateral movement into other privileged systems."

  Surrounding context from the same advisory that the model should consume as corroborating features: initial
  access via default credentials on an SSL-VPN appliance with no account lockout [T1078.001/T1078.002]; OpenSSH
  downloaded and used for tunnelling persistence [T1105/T1572]; SSL-VPN traffic manipulation to harvest
  credentials and session cookies from a VDI portal [T1040] followed by session hijacking [T1539]; deliberate
  operation between 22:00 and 06:00; and deletion of access logs and command history along the way
  [T1685/T1070.003].

  CATEGORY: Alchemy. Flames rejected — the one discrete artifact (LogonType 9 + `seclogo`) is source-side and
  tool-specific, so it is both suppressible by the adversary and absent entirely for the Impacket tradecraft
  this advisory documents; it is retained as a confirming feature, not as the hunt. Embers was a real contender
  and is the closest neighbour to this hunt: [[B037]] baselines WinRM as a set-membership problem, and one could
  argue NTLM authentication is the same. It was rejected because the scale is different in kind — WinRM
  initiation involves tens of hosts and produces a stable edge list, whereas domain-wide NTLM authentication
  involves every host and every account, the edge set never fully converges, and first-time edges occur legitimately
  in large numbers every day. That is precisely the case the rubric names: rule-based detection too noisy to be
  operational at scale, requiring graph and sequence analysis over lateral-movement chains. Hence scoring, not a
  lookup table.

  WHY THIS NEEDS A MODEL AND NOT A RULE. The standard published detection for pass-the-hash is a 4624 with
  LogonType 9, AuthenticationPackageName Negotiate, and LogonProcessName `seclogo`. It is genuinely
  high-fidelity — LogonType 9 is rare and `seclogo` is a strong discriminator. It also has two structural
  problems that make it a poor foundation:
  - IT IS SOURCE-SIDE. LogonType 9 is logged on the machine where the alternate credential is injected — the
    operator's own foothold. That is the one host in the chain they control, can stop forwarding events from,
    can clear logs on, or can simply not have onboarded to your collection in the first place. Building the
    primary detection on the attacker's own telemetry is building on sand.
  - IT IS TOOL-SPECIFIC. `seclogo` reflects a particular credential-injection path (the Secondary Logon route
    that classic Mimikatz `sekurlsa::pth` uses). Impacket's `secretsdump` and its relatives — which is what Gunra
    used — authenticate over the wire from Linux and never produce a LogonType 9 on any Windows host at all.
  On the DESTINATION side, where telemetry is reliable, a pass-the-hash logon is a 4624 LogonType 3 with NTLM,
  and it is byte-identical to the millions of legitimate NTLM network logons in the estate. There is no field to
  match on. What is left is the shape of the graph.

  THE MODEL:
  - UNIT OF SCORING: the authentication EDGE — the (source host/IP, account, destination host) triple — plus a
    per-account aggregate over the window. Score both; the edge catches the individual move, the aggregate
    catches the sweep.
  - WINDOW: rolling 60 minutes for burst and fan-out features; 90 days of authentication history for the
    baseline.
  - FEATURE 1 — EDGE NOVELTY (highest weight): has this exact (source, account, destination) triple ever been
    observed in 90 days? Then the weaker forms: has this ACCOUNT ever authenticated to this DESTINATION? Has
    this SOURCE ever authenticated to this destination at all? A first-ever triple is the atomic unit of lateral
    movement and it is the feature an operator cannot avoid generating — they are, by definition, going
    somewhere the credential has not been.
  - FEATURE 2 — FAN-OUT: distinct destinations reached by one account from one source in the window, scored
    against that account's own historical distribution. A dumped hash is used against many hosts because the
    operator does not yet know which ones matter. Note this must be per-account-relative: an account that
    normally touches two hosts reaching fifteen is extreme; a scanner reaching four thousand is Tuesday.
  - FEATURE 3 — PROTOCOL CHOICE IN CONTEXT: NTLM used where Kerberos was available and is normally used for
    this account/destination pair. Compute the destination's Kerberos capability from 4768/4769 history first —
    NTLM to a host that has never done Kerberos carries no signal, while NTLM to a host that does Kerberos for
    every other account is a strong one. This is the feature that distinguishes hash-passing from ticket-passing
    and from normal authentication, and it is why the negative space (absence of 4768/4769) is a required data
    source.
  - FEATURE 4 — PRIVILEGE ELEVATION ON ARRIVAL: 4672 accompanying the new edge, and whether the account is a
    member of a privileged group or is a local administrator on the destination. This does not detect; it ranks.
  - FEATURE 5 — BURST STRUCTURE: inter-authentication interval variance across the fan-out. Tooling sweeps at
    machine cadence; administrators do not. Low variance across many novel edges is close to conclusive.
  - FEATURE 6 — FAILURE RATIO: 4625s interleaved with the 4624s. An operator spraying a recovered hash across
    hosts where the account has no rights generates a distinctive success/failure mix that legitimate
    administration does not. The failures are frequently a cleaner signal than the successes, and they are
    routinely discarded from ingest.
  - FEATURE 7 — UPSTREAM COUPLING (near-decisive when present): NTDS or DC credential-access activity in the
    preceding hours — Sysmon EID 10 handle opens against lsass.exe on a DC, `ntdsutil` or VSS shadow copy
    creation on a DC, Directory Service Replication rights exercised by a non-DC principal (4662 with the
    DS-Replication-Get-Changes GUIDs), or Impacket-style SMB session-setup patterns. A fan-out that follows an
    NTDS dump is a different event from a fan-out that does not.
  - FEATURE 8 — SOURCE-SIDE CONFIRMATION (bonus, never required): 4624 LogonType 9 with LogonProcessName
    `seclogo` on the source, or Sysmon EID 10 against lsass.exe. When present, treat as near-conclusive. When
    absent, infer nothing — see the structural problems above.
  - SCORING: score each feature against the relevant historical distribution — empirical percentile or z-score —
    and combine multiplicatively so JOINT extremity is required. An account high on fan-out alone is a scanner;
    an account high on novelty AND fan-out AND protocol-choice AND burst structure, hours after a DC credential
    event, is the intrusion.
  - OUTPUT: a ranked review queue of (account, window) clusters with the contributing edges and feature values
    shown, so the analyst sees the movement path rather than a score. Render it as a graph where possible — the
    shape of a lateral-movement fan is recognisable to a human in seconds and is the strongest argument this
    model can make for itself.

  VALIDATION: replay against a known-clean 90-day window and confirm the top of the queue is enumerable, nameable
  identities — vulnerability scanners, backup agents, config management. If it is not, those populations are not
  modelled yet and the baseline work is incomplete. Then, with authorisation, have a red team perform a
  pass-the-hash fan-out from a host outside the normal administrative set and confirm it ranks above the scanner
  population. If it does not, novelty is underweighted relative to fan-out.

  ALSO DEPLOY THE STATIC RULE. The LogonType 9 / `seclogo` detection costs nothing, catches the Mimikatz-style
  case outright, and provides Feature 8. This model is the layer that catches the Impacket case it structurally
  cannot see — not a replacement for it.

  CROSS-REF: T1550.003 (Pass the Ticket — the sibling technique from the same advisory sentence; it produces
  Kerberos rather than NTLM authentication, so Feature 3 inverts, but every other feature in this model applies
  unchanged and both should be scored in one pass), T1003.003 (NTDS — the upstream event that makes this
  possible and the strongest corroborating feature), T1021.002 (SMB/Admin Shares — what the authenticated session
  is usually used for; the 5140/5145 follow-through separates an authentication from an actual move),
  T1078.002 (Domain Accounts), T1550.001 (Application Access Token) for the cloud-side equivalent.
  [[M034]] covers the cloud-storage collection from the same advisory. [[B037]] baselines WinRM, one of the
  services these recovered credentials get spent on.

# M035

Once `secretsdump.py` has run against a domain controller, the operator has every hash in the estate. They no longer need a password to authenticate anywhere — and the authentication they produce is cryptographically valid, correctly formed, and indistinguishable from the millions of legitimate NTLM logons your domain generates every day.

The standard detection is a 4624 with LogonType 9 and logon process `seclogo`. It is a good rule and it has two structural problems. It is logged on the *source* host — the operator's own foothold, the one machine they control and can stop forwarding from. And it reflects a specific credential-injection path that Impacket, which is what Gunra actually used, never touches. Authenticating over the wire from Linux produces no LogonType 9 on any Windows host at all.

On the destination side, where the telemetry is reliable, there is nothing to match on. A passed hash is a LogonType 3 NTLM network logon. So is a printer.

What is left is the graph. This account has never authenticated to these fifteen hosts. This source has never spoken to any of them. All of it happened in eleven minutes, over NTLM, in an estate where this account negotiates Kerberos everywhere else — three hours after something opened a handle to LSASS on a domain controller.

## Why

- **The high-fidelity artifact lives on the attacker's machine, and that is not a foundation.** LogonType 9 with `seclogo` is genuinely rare and genuinely diagnostic — and it is generated on the host where the operator injected the credential. That host may not be onboarded, may not be forwarding, may have had its logs cleared, or may be a Linux box running Impacket that never produced the event in the first place. Gunra deleted access logs and cleared command history as standard practice. Any model that depends on source-side telemetry is depending on the one part of the estate the adversary controls; the destination-side graph is the part they cannot reach.

- **Novelty is the feature the technique cannot avoid generating.** Lateral movement means going somewhere this credential has not been — that is what the words mean. The operator can pace their timing, limit their fan-out, prefer Kerberos, and avoid every tool signature, and they still create authentication edges that did not exist in the previous ninety days, because their entire purpose is to reach hosts they have not yet reached. Scoring first-ever (source, account, destination) triples targets the objective rather than any implementation of it, and it is the feature that survives a careful operator.

- **Per-account baselining is what makes fan-out usable, and a global threshold makes it useless.** Fan-out is the intuitive signal and it fails immediately in a real estate: vulnerability scanners, backup agents, and configuration management authenticate to thousands of hosts on a schedule, at a scale no operator approaches. A global threshold either sits above the scanners and misses everything, or sits below them and returns the scanners forever. Scoring fan-out against each account's *own* distribution makes "normal for a scanner" and "normal for a helpdesk account" separate statements — and an account that normally touches two hosts reaching fifteen becomes extreme without any absolute number being chosen.

- **Protocol choice only means something in context, and computing that context is the difference between a signal and noise.** "NTLM was used" is not evidence — a third of the NTLM in most estates is someone connecting by IP address instead of hostname, and appliances and legacy applications cannot do Kerberos at all. But NTLM to a destination that services Kerberos for every *other* account, by an account that uses Kerberos everywhere else, is a real anomaly. That requires deriving each destination's Kerberos capability from 4768/4769 history first, which is why the absence of Kerberos events is listed as a required data source rather than an afterthought.

- **The failures are often cleaner than the successes, and they are usually thrown away.** An operator spraying a recovered hash across hosts where the account has no local rights generates a distinctive interleaving of 4625s and 4624s that legitimate administration does not produce — administrators generally know where their accounts work. Many pipelines drop 4625 at ingest for volume reasons, discarding one of the better-shaped features available. Retaining it costs storage and buys a discriminator that is hard for an operator to suppress, because the failures are a consequence of not knowing the estate.

- **Upstream coupling turns a ranked anomaly into an incident.** A fan-out of novel NTLM edges is suspicious. A fan-out of novel NTLM edges three hours after a non-DC principal exercised Directory Service Replication rights, or after a handle was opened against LSASS on a domain controller, or after a VSS shadow copy appeared on a DC, is the advisory's chain reproducing itself in your telemetry. Correlating the credential-access event with the movement that follows is the single highest-value feature in the model, and both halves are events most estates already collect but rarely join.

- **Rendering the output as a graph is not decoration — it is what makes the model trusted.** A score attached to an account tells an analyst nothing they can act on. The fan of edges from one source, through one account, to fifteen destinations in eleven minutes is recognisable as lateral movement in about two seconds, by anyone. Models that produce numbers get ignored; models that produce a picture of the movement path get worked. That is a deliberate design requirement here, not a presentation preference.

- **HEARTH had no coverage for T1550.002 or T1550.003,** despite pass-the-hash being the standard consequence of every NTDS dump and the mechanism by which a single domain-controller compromise becomes an estate-wide one.
