false_positive_notes: |
  The benign population that matches the central feature is large and completely ordinary. A mismatch between `Reply-To` and `From` is normal and widespread - marketing platforms, ticketing and helpdesk systems, applicant-tracking and CRM tools, newsletter senders, transactional mail services and no-reply infrastructure all set a different reply address by design. On its own, that feature is nearly useless. It becomes discriminating only in combination, and specifically when the `Reply-To` domain is also newly registered AND the display name matches an executive while the sender is external. Newly registered domains have their own benign tail - genuine new vendors, rebrands, acquisitions, campaign microsites and small suppliers that have just incorporated - and a new supplier sending a first invoice is the single most awkward false positive here, because it is legitimately a first-seen sender with a young domain asking about payment. Executive display-name matching also fires on real executives sending from personal accounts while travelling, on external namesakes, and on board members and advisors with external addresses, so fuzzy-match thresholds need to be set conservatively and reviewed. Two cautions about feature durability. The AI-authorship tells - HTML comments describing sections, capitalised section headers with heavy style-element commenting, em dashes, `==========` banner lines - are a fast-decaying signal class, and they will decay further as generation tooling improves and as legitimate business mail becomes more AI-assisted, which it already is. They must never gate a detection; they are aggregate weak evidence at best and should be reviewed for removal every quarter. The fabricated-thread check is more durable but noisy, because legitimate forwards are frequently reconstructed by mobile clients in ways that break header continuity. Finally, an empty result is ambiguous in a specific and important way - if `ReplyToAddress` is not populated in your tenant, or an upstream gateway rewrote headers before Microsoft 365 received the message, this hunt cannot return anything regardless of what is arriving. Verify the field is present on known-good mail before interpreting silence, and note that silence in email telemetry is also entirely compatible with a successful fraud, since the loss happens in the finance system where security has no visibility at all.
notes: |
  SCOPING, state this plainly before anyone starts - there is NO mailbox compromise in this campaign. No account takeover, no stolen session, no inbox rule, no auto-forwarding, no post-delivery adversary activity, and no endpoint involvement whatsoever. Every piece of attacker infrastructure is external to the tenant. That makes this a MAIL-ANALYTICS hunt rather than an intrusion hunt, and the delivered message is the only observable the defender has. T1657 Financial Theft is realised here as a finance-process outcome - an ACH transfer - which is not visible in security telemetry at any point. The fraud completes in the accounts-payable system. This is exactly why detection has to happen at the email layer, and why the most valuable output of the hunt is a reconciliation with the finance team's payment-change process rather than an alert queue.
  Campaign facts from the source - more than one million emails delivered between 3 and 5 August 2026, 87.7 percent of them to United States recipients, targeting IT services, business advisory and consumer goods organisations. The supporting infrastructure was registered on 31 July, three days before the send - `service-nowinc[.]com`, a ServiceNow lookalike, and `domainlify[.]net`. One confirmed loss, an ACH transfer of approximately 50,000 US dollars. There is NO KQL and NO Sigma in the source article - the queries and weights in this hunt are constructed from the reported structure, not reproduced from Microsoft.
  The structural pattern, which is the model's core feature - the `From` domain is NOT the lookalike. Senders were mostly legitimate-looking or compromised third-party domains, observed as `gomez@service-nowinc[.]com`, `notifications@uinsure[.]co[.]uk`, `info@tivityhealth[.]com`, `no-reply@lumalisboa[.]com`, `noreply@mctci[.]com`, `info@nuf[.]co[.]jp`, `info@lohnsteuerhilfe-aktuell-verein[.]de`, `info@tovimbatista[.]pt`, `contact@eemusicclass[.]co[.]uk` and `info@lifeones[.]com`. The lookalike domain sits in the `Reply-To` header. Mail authentication therefore does not catch this and cannot be expected to - SPF, DKIM and DMARC all validate the SENDING domain, which in this campaign is a real domain that may pass every check perfectly, while the reply is routed somewhere else entirely. A message can be fully authenticated and still be this attack. That gap is the reason the hunt exists.
  Additional scorable features - a FAKE FORWARDED THREAD, being two quoted messages simulating a prior executive conversation that authorised the payment, which lack authentic email headers, meaning the quoted forward blocks have no corresponding `Received` or `Message-ID` chain to support them. And AI-GENERATION TELLS in the HTML invoice body - extensive HTML comments describing each section, verbose capitalised section headers accompanied by excessive style-element comments, em dashes, and `==========` banner lines - across a uniform template in which only the organisation-specific details varied between targets. Treat both as weak individual features that are useful only in aggregate, and treat the AI tells in particular as a decaying signal class that must never gate on its own.
  Model shape - a per-message risk score over INBOUND EXTERNAL mail, weighted as follows. HEAVY - (1) `ReplyToAddress` domain differs from `SenderFromAddress` domain; (2) `Reply-To` domain registration age under 30 days, with under 7 days scoring at the top, since the campaign domains were 3 days old; (3) `SenderDisplayName` fuzzy-matching an executive or finance-authority name in the Entra directory while `SenderFromAddress` is external, which is the impersonation core and the feature most tightly bound to the adversary's actual goal. MEDIUM - (4) first-time sender domain for the organisation; (5) an invoice, remittance or payment-instruction attachment or body; (6) bank-detail strings present - ACH, routing number, IBAN, SWIFT, account number - scoring highest where those details DIFFER from the vendor's recorded details in the finance system; (7) the fake-thread header anomaly, quoted forward blocks with no supporting `Received` or `Message-ID` chain. LIGHT - (8) HTML and AI template tells. The score is multiplicative between groups 1 to 3 and additive within the rest - a message that trips all three heavy features should outrank anything that trips only the medium ones, however many.
  Output shape and triage - and this is the part that makes the hunt different, so read it before building anything - route high scores to a FINANCE VERIFICATION workflow, not only to the SOC. The output is a ranked list of inbound messages carrying sender address, sender display name, the matched executive name and match distance, `Reply-To` address and its domain age, first-seen status, attachment and URL detail, extracted bank-detail strings, and the score breakdown by feature. The SOC disposition is limited - block the domain, ZAP the message, warn the recipients - because there is no host to isolate and no account to reset. The finance disposition is where the actual risk is controlled. The single highest-value control this hunt produces is an out-of-band callback procedure for ANY payment-detail change, verified against a phone number already on file rather than one supplied in the message, and the hunt should be considered to have succeeded if that procedure exists at the end of it even if no fraudulent mail is found.
  Collection gap to fix as an output - `ReplyToAddress` is the pivotal field and is frequently absent. Confirm it is populated in `EmailEvents` for known-good inbound mail, confirm no upstream gateway is rewriting or stripping reply headers before Microsoft 365, and if enhanced filtering for connectors is in use, confirm the original sender information is being preserved rather than replaced by the gateway hop. A tenant that cannot see `Reply-To` is structurally blind to this campaign class.
  Microsoft mitigations to cite and check as part of the hunt - correct SPF, DKIM and DMARC configuration on your own domains, understanding that this campaign is not defeated by them; zero-hour auto purge, which retroactively removes delivered phishing within a 48-hour window and is the main post-delivery lever available; enhanced filtering for connectors in Exchange Online, so that mail arriving via a third-party gateway retains its true source IP and sender information rather than adopting the gateway's; and automatic attack disruption.
  Why this is Alchemy rather than Flames - no single feature here is a detection. Reply-To divergence describes most marketing mail. Newly registered domains include every genuine new vendor. Executive display names appear on legitimate external mail. Payment language appears in every invoice the organisation receives. Each feature individually has a benign base rate so high that a rule built on it is unusable, and the campaign was specifically constructed so that the one control that should have caught it - mail authentication - passes cleanly. Detection therefore requires weighting several weak, individually-benign features into a score and calibrating it against the organisation's own mail, its own executive names and its own vendor population. It is also unusual in that the correct consumer of the output is the finance function rather than the SOC, which is itself a reason it does not belong in Flames.

# M047

Microsoft's report on AI-assisted executive impersonation describes a campaign that delivered more than a million emails over three days in August 2026, almost 88 percent of them into United States organisations in IT services, business advisory and consumer goods, and which produced at least one confirmed ACH loss of around fifty thousand dollars. What makes it worth a hunt is not the volume but the construction. There is no intrusion. Nobody's mailbox was taken over, no session was stolen, no inbox rule was created, nothing was forwarded, and no endpoint was touched - every piece of attacker infrastructure sat outside the tenant, and the only thing that ever crossed the boundary was a message. The clever part is where the lookalike domain lives. The `From` addresses were real or compromised third-party domains that could pass SPF, DKIM and DMARC without difficulty, because they genuinely are the domains they claim to be. The ServiceNow lookalike, `service-nowinc[.]com`, and the supporting `domainlify[.]net` - both registered on 31 July, three days before the send - appear in the `Reply-To` header, which mail authentication does not validate and was never designed to. A fully authenticated message routes its reply to the adversary. On top of that sits a fabricated forwarded thread implying an executive had already approved the payment, and an AI-generated HTML invoice from a uniform template in which only the target's own details changed. The fraud itself then completes somewhere security has no visibility at all: in accounts payable.

## Why

- **The control that should have stopped this passes it cleanly, by design.** SPF, DKIM and DMARC authenticate the sending domain. This campaign's sending domains are real, so they authenticate. The domain that matters is the one the reply goes to, and nothing in mail authentication looks at `Reply-To`. Any organisation that treats a DMARC pass as evidence of legitimacy is structurally exposed to this, and saying so is half the value of writing the hunt down.
- **Every feature is benign alone and the campaign is only visible in their combination.** Reply-To divergence describes most marketing mail; newly registered domains describe every new vendor; executive names appear on legitimate external correspondence; invoice language appears in every genuine invoice. There is no threshold on any one of them that yields a usable rule, which is precisely the condition that calls for a weighted score calibrated on the organisation's own mail rather than a signature.
- **The impersonation triple is the part that survives rotation, so it should carry the weight.** Sending domains, lookalike strings, subject lines and body wording are all cheap for the adversary to change, and a campaign that sends a million messages in three days will have changed all of them by the next one. What does not change without abandoning the scheme is that the display name must impersonate someone the recipient trusts, the reply must reach the adversary rather than that person, and the reply domain must be recent because the scheme is recent.
- **The AI-generation tells are real evidence with a short shelf life, and pretending otherwise will age the hunt badly.** HTML comments narrating each section, capitalised headers, em dashes and banner lines were genuinely present and genuinely distinguishing in August 2026. They will not be in a year, both because generation tooling improves and because legitimate business correspondence is already increasingly AI-assisted. Score them lightly, never gate on them, and schedule a review to remove them.
- **The most valuable output is a process, not an alert, because the loss does not happen in telemetry.** T1657 Financial Theft is realised as an ACH transfer in the finance system, invisible to every security tool in the estate - which is why T1657 is so rarely hunted and why coverage for it is thin. The defensible outcome of this work is a reconciliation against the finance team's payment-change log and an out-of-band callback procedure for every bank-detail change, verified on a number already held on file. That control holds even when the next campaign looks nothing like this one.
