# B062

The adversary is not breaking in. They are being hired.

FAMOUS CHOLLIMA operatives take remote jobs under fraudulent identities, have the employer-issued laptop shipped to a facilitator in the United States, and work it remotely from abroad. CrowdStrike's Counter Adversary Operations team worked with the FBI in late September 2026 to disrupt several of these laptop farms, and the victim organisations span aerospace, energy, financial services, healthcare, cybersecurity, defense engineering, government technology and consumer technology — "FAMOUS CHOLLIMA's sector-agnostic targeting posture, in which any organization offering remote employment provides an opportunity for revenue generation."

What makes this a baseline problem rather than a detection problem is the specific evasion CrowdStrike describes.

Since May 2026 the group has been spoofing device hashes — the example given is "making a video capture card appear as a webcam." A capture card is how you feed a video signal from somewhere else into a machine so it looks like a local camera. Presenting it as a webcam makes it invisible to anyone looking for capture hardware.

Now ask how you would detect that as an event. You cannot. A device announcing itself as a webcam is not anomalous. It is only anomalous against knowledge of which webcams your fleet actually ships with, which models appear on your standard image, and what the device descriptor normally looks like on the hardware you buy. That knowledge is an inventory, and most organisations do not have it.

The second observation has the same shape. OverWatch saw "the same disguised device across multiple separate victim organizations, suggesting operatives are deliberately reusing device identifiers to evade detection", and found laptop-farm network infrastructure "sharing identifiers across multiple concurrent fake personas." A repeated identifier is meaningless without a record of what has been seen before. That record is also an inventory.

The software half is the same story again. A facilitator installs hardware and software to enable remote access, and CrowdStrike notes the group now tries to conceal that tooling, "likely due to increased operational security following public reporting on their activity." Remote-access tooling is legitimately everywhere — help desks, developers, anyone who records their screen. Whether a given instance is sanctioned is an inventory question, not a signature question.

So collect first. The hunts this enables are listed at the end, and none of them can run without it.

One honest limitation up front: CrowdStrike's strongest observation — identifier reuse *across organisations* — is not something any single organisation can see from its own telemetry. The within-fleet version of that question is what this baseline answers, and it is still worth answering.

## Baseline data to collect

Collect per endpoint, for every remote-worker and remote-eligible Windows device in scope.

**Peripheral device inventory.** For every PnP device connection event: hostname, logged-on user, timestamp of arrival and removal, device class GUID and friendly class name, device description as reported, manufacturer string, vendor ID and product ID, the full device instance path including any serial component, and the driver service backing it. The descriptor-versus-class comparison is the whole point, so record the reported description and the enumerated class as separate fields rather than collapsing them.

**Device identifier recurrence.** For each distinct vendor ID, product ID and instance-path serial: the set of hostnames it has appeared on, the set of users, the first and last time seen, and the count of distinct hosts. This is the table that answers whether an identifier is unique to one machine or recurring across several.

**Hardware reference set.** From asset management: the model of each endpoint, its standard image version, the shipping address it was sent to, the assigned user and that user's reported work location. The shipping-address field matters because CrowdStrike lists "Discrepancies between the residential address and shipping address" among the screening indicators.

**Remote-access, KVM and streaming software.** For each endpoint: every remote-access, remote-desktop, KVM-over-IP, screen-recording and commercial-VPN product present, whether installed or run portably; the install path; the signer and signature validity; the install or first-execution date; and whether it appears in the standard image or the sanctioned software list. CrowdStrike names "Astrill VPN, AnyDesk, OBS Studio, or PiKVM" as examples; collect the category rather than the list, because the named products are illustrative.

**Egress for each of those tools.** Destination addresses and hostnames, ports, and the volume and timing profile per tool per endpoint. A sanctioned RMM agent talks to one tenant; an unsanctioned one talks somewhere else.

**Sign-in location and timing per identity.** Source IP, geolocation, device ID and timestamp for every sign-in, summarised to the distinct locations per identity per day and the local-time-of-day distribution. CrowdStrike lists "Logins to multiple locations during time zones that do not match the reported location" as a screening indicator, and the comparison requires knowing the reported location.

## Collection window

Thirty days of continuous collection, which is long enough to capture a full monthly work cycle including travel, on-call rotations and month-end activity, and which matches the Defender XDR advanced hunting retention limit so the baseline can be rebuilt from telemetry rather than only accumulated forward.

Extend to sixty days for the device-identifier recurrence table specifically, where that history is available from a CMDB or SIEM with longer retention. Recurrence is a question about history, and more history gives a better answer.

Re-run the full collection quarterly, and re-run it unconditionally after any hardware refresh, standard-image change or onboarding wave, since each of those resets what "normal" means.

## Deliverable

Four artefacts.

**A fleet hardware allowlist** — every device class, vendor ID and product ID legitimately present, with the models and standard-image versions they belong to, the expected device description for each, and a named owner for every exception. An entry should let a reviewer answer "should this machine have this device" without further research.

**A device-identifier uniqueness register** — every identifier observed, the hosts and users it appeared on, and a flag for every identifier seen on more than one host. Virtual machine, cloned-image, thin-client and virtual-driver identifiers are enumerated and excluded here once, by name, so that the remaining duplicates are genuinely interesting.

**A sanctioned remote-access and streaming inventory** — per product: whether it is approved, which population may have it, its expected egress destinations, its expected signer, and its install provenance. Everything present but unsanctioned goes on an exception list with an owner and a decision date.

**A per-identity location and timing profile** — the normal set of sign-in locations and the normal local-hour distribution for each remote worker, recorded alongside the location that worker reports, so a mismatch is a comparison against a stated value rather than an impression.

## Flag immediately — do not wait for the baseline to complete

These do not need thirty days of history to be worth investigating today.

- A device whose reported description contradicts its enumerated hardware class — the capture-card-as-webcam case CrowdStrike names, and the inverse.
- A video capture device of any kind on an endpoint whose role does not involve video production, especially where a webcam is already present.
- A KVM-over-IP device, or a USB device presenting as both a keyboard and a storage or network interface, on a standard-issue corporate laptop.
- The same device instance identifier or serial appearing on two or more endpoints that should each have unique hardware, after the known virtual and cloned-image exclusions are applied.
- Remote-access, KVM or commercial-VPN software installed outside the standard image on an endpoint whose assigned user has no help-desk or engineering role.
- Remote-access software that is present but whose process, service or scheduled task has been renamed, relocated outside its install directory, or has its autostart entry removed while the binary remains — CrowdStrike notes the group attempts to hide this tooling.
- Sign-ins for one identity from locations that cannot be reconciled in the elapsed time, or whose local working hours are inconsistent with the worker's stated location.
- An endpoint whose shipping address in asset management does not match the assigned user's residential address of record.
- Any endpoint where new peripheral hardware appeared within the first week of employment and has no procurement record.

## Cross-references

This baseline is what makes several behavioural hunts answerable.

[[H307]] covers an endpoint enrolled into an attacker-controlled software deployment tenant and then scripted from it — the egress profile per remote-management tool collected here is the reference data that makes an unexpected tenant visible. [[H306]] covers a remote-support client spawning a script interpreter, where the sanctioned-tooling inventory determines whether the client should have been present at all. [[B045]] baselines every Windows process that asks the internet what its own external IP is, which pairs with the sign-in location profile here: a remote worker's apparent location can be cross-read from both identity telemetry and endpoint behaviour.

The device-identifier register is also the prerequisite for any future Flames hunt on hardware-identity spoofing. Until the register exists, such a hunt has nothing to compare against.

## Why

- **The evasion is defined by contradiction, not by signature.** A capture card presenting as a webcam produces no anomalous event; it is only visible against a record of what hardware the fleet legitimately has.
- **Identifier reuse is a question about history.** CrowdStrike observed the same disguised device across multiple organisations, and a recurring identifier means nothing without a register of what has been seen before.
- **The adversary is deliberately hiding the software half.** OverWatch notes attempts to conceal RMM tooling following public reporting, so presence alone is a weak signal and sanctioned-versus-unsanctioned is the real question.
- **Remote-access tooling is too common to alert on.** Help desks, developers and anyone who records their screen legitimately run it, which makes an inventory the only way to make an exception list meaningful.
- **Screening indicators need a reference value.** Location and address mismatches are only computable when the reported location and shipping address are recorded alongside the observed ones.
- **Indicators here are explicitly transient.** CrowdStrike states that maintaining a list of known personas and indicators "is useful, these are highly transient" — which makes the structural inventory the durable asset.
- **Targeting is sector-agnostic.** Any organisation offering remote employment is in scope, so exposure follows hiring model rather than industry or perceived threat profile.
- **Duplicate-identifier queries fail without exclusions.** Virtual machines, cloned images and virtual drivers produce duplicates by design, and enumerating them once is what makes the remaining duplicates legible.
- **Disruption does not end the campaign.** CrowdStrike assesses the takedown "is unlikely to create long-lasting effects" and that operations are ongoing, so the baseline retains its value well past this news cycle.
