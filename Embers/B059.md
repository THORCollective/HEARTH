false_positive_notes: |
  The failure mode for this baseline is not noise, it is staleness, and CI/CD estates drift faster than almost anything else. Pipelines are created and retired weekly, teams reorganise, service connections accumulate, and a baseline captured once and never refreshed will be wrong within a month and will then be ignored - which is worse than not having one, because its existence discourages anyone from asking the question again. Refresh on a schedule and treat every diff as either an approved change or a finding, with no third category. The second problem is that legitimate breadth exists and must be recorded rather than flagged - platform, SRE and release-engineering teams genuinely own pipelines that touch many resources, and cluster-bootstrap, secret-distribution, disaster-recovery and migration pipelines genuinely read many credentials by design. These are not exceptions to be suppressed; they are the highest-value entries in the inventory, because they are what an attacker would most want to take over, and they should carry a named owner and a review date. Third, authoring-history scope needs care - a new engineer's first legitimate pipeline commit looks exactly like an attacker's, so the baseline unit should be the *team* or group that owns a project rather than the individual, with individual novelty as a weaker secondary signal. Fourth, monorepos and templated pipelines complicate attribution, since a change to a shared template propagates to many definitions without anyone editing those definitions; record template-to-definition relationships explicitly or the diff will misattribute the change. Finally, note the ordering trap - a baseline captured after a compromise records the attacker's pipeline as normal. Where an identity is known to have been compromised, reconstruct that project's history from the audit log and Git rather than snapshotting current state.

  Platform: Azure DevOps (SaaS) primarily, with the same structure applying to GitHub Actions, GitLab CI and Jenkins. Driven by the Microsoft Security Blog, "Beyond source code - A path to the keys to the kingdom" (29 September 2026), summarising a Microsoft DART Cyberattack Series report on Storm-3068.
  Why the baseline is the precondition. Microsoft's own summary of the lesson is the argument for this hunt - "when identities, source code, pipelines, and production environments are tightly linked, a single account compromise can provide a pathway to much broader access across the organization," and "repositories, pipelines, service connections, and deployment settings can provide threat actors with a roadmap to an organization's broader environment." The attacker read that roadmap by enumerating it, using "legitimate administrative tools and automated scripts to enumerate repositories, projects, pipelines, and deployment environments." The defender's position is weaker than the attacker's precisely because the attacker did the enumeration and the defender did not. Microsoft's recommendations - "controlling pipeline permissions and limiting who can create, modify, or execute build and deployment pipelines" and "regular reviews of identity, DevOps, and cloud security controls" - are both unactionable without an inventory, because you cannot limit or review what has never been counted.
  Collection fields, grouped by the question each one answers.
  - Authorship. Per pipeline definition - the identities that have created or modified it, with dates, from Git history and the platform audit log. Record the owning team or group, not just individuals. This is what makes "a pipeline edited by someone who has never edited one here" a usable signal in [[H373]], and the team-level unit is what keeps a new hire from generating a finding on their first week.
  - Entitlement breadth. Per pipeline - the count and identity of every resource, service connection, environment, variable group, secret store and agent pool it is authorised to use. Rank the whole estate by this number descending. Storm-3068's pipeline reached more than fifty resources; the useful output is not detecting that one, it is knowing which of yours are comparable.
  - Credential entitlement. Per pipeline - which secrets, service principals, cluster credentials and key vault entries it may read, and which of those grant production access. Map the inverse too - per credential, the set of pipelines entitled to it - because that is the blast radius of a single secret and it is the view nobody has.
  - Output profile. Per pipeline - what it normally produces. Published artifacts, test runs, deployment targets. A definition with a credential-read step and no artifact, test or deployment is the purpose-mismatch signal [[H373]] relies on, and it only reads as mismatched against a recorded normal.
  - Execution surface. Per pipeline - the agent pools it may run on, whether any are self-hosted, and the identity and network position of each self-hosted agent host. Self-hosted runners are endpoints inside the estate with production credentials, and they are frequently absent from the asset inventory entirely.
  - Approval and protection state. Per pipeline and environment - required approvals, branch protection, whether pipeline changes require review, and whether secrets are available to pull-request builds from forks. These are the controls that would have constrained this intrusion, and recording their current state is the same work as auditing them.
  - Identity rights. The set of identities holding pipeline create, modify, execute and approve rights per project, and their Entra group derivation. Join this against which of those identities are privileged elsewhere and which are exposed to self-service password reset - the intrusion began with SSPR followed by the actor registering its own authentication methods.
  Collection window and cadence. Capture the full inventory once, then refresh weekly, with an immediate refresh triggered by any pipeline permission change or service connection creation. Keep thirty days of pipeline run history for the output-profile baseline, and retain authorship history for the life of the repository since Git already does. Two refresh cycles should pass before the diff job is trusted, so that normal weekly churn can be separated from drift.
  Deliverable format. Per project, a signed snapshot stored off-platform containing - the pipeline-to-author-team map; the pipeline-to-resource and pipeline-to-service-connection entitlement lists with counts; the inverse credential-to-pipeline map; the per-pipeline output profile; the agent pool and self-hosted runner inventory; the approval and protection state; and the identity rights table. Plus a scheduled diff job reporting new or modified definitions with their authors, entitlement increases, new service connections, and any pipeline whose output profile changed. Plus one ranked report - every pipeline by resource-authorisation count - which is the blast-radius backlog.
  Immediate flags - do not wait for the baseline to complete. (1) Any pipeline definition created or modified by an identity with no prior authoring history in that project. (2) Credential material committed to any repository - search Git history, not the working tree, since Microsoft's investigators reconstructed the theft of seven `kubeconfig` files from version history after the fact. (3) A pipeline run that reads secrets or cluster credentials and publishes no artifact, runs no test and deploys nothing. (4) Installation of remote-management software or a tunnelling utility from a pipeline task - Storm-3068 used Atera and Chisel. (5) An outbound long-lived connection from a build agent to an external address. (6) A new service connection authorised against production resources, created outside a change record. (7) A pipeline permission or resource-authorisation change made within an hour of a password reset or authentication-method registration on the same identity. (8) Secrets made available to pull-request builds from forks.
  Enables. [[H373]] (malicious pipeline harvesting cluster credentials) directly. [[H329]] covers the authentication-method registration that precedes this class of intrusion from a different initial vector, and the identity-rights field here is what connects the two. [[B057]] and [[B058]] are the sibling baselines for internet-facing web appliances and Linux network-edge devices; all three share the same discipline - signed, off-platform, tagged snapshots plus a diff job - and should share storage and tagging conventions.

# B059

Storm-3068's pipeline was authorised to reach more than fifty resources. The number is the finding, and almost nobody could produce it for their own estate.

Microsoft's DART write-up of the intrusion is careful to note there was no malware and no exploit. An account was taken over through a self-service password reset, the actor registered its own authentication methods to keep it, and then it went to Azure DevOps and enumerated — repositories, projects, pipelines, deployment environments. Microsoft's phrasing is worth keeping: repositories, pipelines, service connections and deployment settings "can provide threat actors with a roadmap to an organization's broader environment."

The uncomfortable part is that the attacker built that roadmap and the defender had not. Every subsequent step was a legitimate capability exercised by someone who should not have had it. A pipeline was created. It read credentials. It deployed an agent into a cluster. Those are the three things pipelines exist to do. There is no action in the sequence that is anomalous in isolation, which means detection has to come from entitlement and provenance — and both are inventory problems.

So this baseline asks four questions per pipeline, none of which any CI platform answers on its own:

**Who normally authors this?** Not to flag individuals, but to make "edited by someone with no authoring history in this project" a usable signal. The unit has to be the owning team, or every new engineer's first commit becomes a finding.

**How much can it reach?** Count the resources, service connections, environments, variable groups and agent pools each definition is authorised against, and rank the estate descending. Microsoft recommends "controlling pipeline permissions and limiting who can create, modify, or execute build and deployment pipelines," which is impossible to act on without that list. The ranked report *is* a hardening backlog, and it is worth more than the hunt it supports.

**Which credentials is it entitled to read?** And then the inverse, which is the view nobody has: for each production secret, which pipelines may read it. That is the blast radius of one credential.

**What does it normally produce?** Because the sharpest signal in [[H373]] is a pipeline that reads secrets and publishes no artifact, runs no test, and deploys nothing. That only reads as wrong against a recorded normal.

One field deserves its own mention. Self-hosted agents are endpoints sitting inside the estate holding production credentials, and they are routinely missing from the asset inventory altogether — which makes a reverse tunnel from one, as Chisel established here, both effective and unmonitored.

And a warning that applies to all three of these baselines: a snapshot taken after a compromise enshrines the attacker's pipeline as normal. Where an identity is known to have been compromised, reconstruct the project's history from the audit log and Git rather than trusting current state.

## Why

- **Nothing in the intrusion was anomalous as an action, so entitlement is the only discriminator left.** No malware, no exploit, no endpoint process execution. Separating a malicious pipeline from a legitimate one requires knowing what each was supposed to be able to do.
- **The attacker enumerated the trust graph and the defender had not.** That asymmetry is the whole lesson. Building the inventory removes the attacker's informational advantage and is the one defensive step that does not depend on catching anything.
- **The ranked breadth report pays for itself independent of any hunt.** A pipeline authorised against fifty resources is a single point of compromise for fifty, whether or not anyone ever attacks it. Counting produces a permanent backlog.
- **The inverse credential map is the view no platform gives you.** Per-pipeline entitlement is visible with effort; per-credential exposure across all pipelines is not, and it is the one that answers "what did we lose."
- **Output profile is what makes purpose-mismatch detectable.** Reading secrets is normal. Reading secrets and producing nothing is collection — but only a recorded baseline of what each pipeline normally emits makes that comparison possible.
- **Team-level authorship keeps the signal usable.** Individual novelty generates a finding every time someone joins. Group ownership with individual novelty as a secondary feature is the version that survives contact with a real engineering organisation.
- **Self-hosted runners are unmanaged endpoints with production credentials.** They are frequently absent from asset inventories, which is precisely why a build-agent reverse tunnel works. Recording them is half the mitigation.
- **Staleness, not noise, is the way this fails.** CI estates drift weekly. An unrefreshed baseline is wrong within a month, and its existence then discourages anyone from asking the question again — so the refresh schedule is part of the control, not an operational detail.
- **Recording the protection state is the same work as auditing it.** Required approvals, branch protection, review-on-change and fork-build secret exposure are the controls that would have constrained this intrusion. Capturing their current state produces the audit as a by-product.
