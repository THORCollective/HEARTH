false_positive_notes: |
  This is an inventory exercise, so the operative question is what belongs in the register rather than what to suppress, and a great deal of what you collect will look alarming while being entirely routine. ScreenConnect, ConnectWise Control, AnyDesk, TeamViewer, NetSupport, N-able and their peers are real commercial products with real licences, and in most organisations at least one of them is genuinely deployed - often by a help desk, an MSP, a field-services team or a single business unit that bought it independently, and frequently without the security team knowing. Expect to find more RMM products than anybody predicted, and expect several of them to be legitimate. The same applies to exclusions - backup agents, line-of-business applications, database engines, developer toolchains, EDA and CAD packages and imaging software all ship vendor guidance recommending specific path or process exclusions, and many of those are correct and necessary. The register exists to record them WITH A JUSTIFICATION AND AN OWNER, not to remove them. Two distinctions do most of the work. For RMM, the durable one is not the product but the RELAY - a legitimate deployment talks to the organisation's own licensed server or to the vendor relay associated with the organisation's own instance, and an instance whose relay FQDN belongs to somebody else is not your instance regardless of how legitimately the binary is signed. ScreenConnect trial relays are especially informative in an environment that pays for a licence, because a paying organisation has no reason to produce trial-relay traffic. For exclusions, the durable one is BREADTH AND PROVENANCE rather than existence - a vendor-recommended exclusion is narrow, specific and set by the management agent or by GPO/Intune, while a drive-root exclusion set locally by an installer running from a user's Downloads folder is self-evidently not that. Two cautions on interpretation. First, an empty or thin exclusion inventory is ambiguous and must be resolved before it is believed - it may mean the estate is clean, or it may mean you are reading only the policy hive and not the local hive, or only the machines your configuration-assessment coverage reaches. Tamper protection also actively prevents some reads and some tooling, which can make a well-protected host look unenumerable. Verify by setting a known test exclusion on a reference host and confirming it appears in every collection path you intend to rely on. Second, absence of a 7045 for an RMM client does not mean the client is absent - portable and memory-only RMM executables exist precisely to avoid service registration, which is why the network-egress axis has to be collected alongside the service axis rather than instead of it.
notes: |
  Collection window - 30 days, running continuously, for the event-driven axes (service installs, Defender configuration changes, RMM egress). Thirty days covers a full monthly cycle including patch Tuesday, monthly maintenance windows, MSP scheduled work and month-end processes, which is when most legitimate RMM and agent activity clusters. The configuration axes - the exclusion lists, tamper-protection and real-time-protection state, and the uninstall-key inventory - are POINT-IN-TIME and should be snapshotted on day one and again on day thirty, because the value of this baseline is the diff. The sanctioned-tooling list is not derived from telemetry at all - collect it on day one from the IT service catalogue, the software asset register and the MSP contracts.
  Why these two inventories sit in one exercise - they answer the same triage question from opposite ends. An RMM client and a Defender exclusion are both legitimate configuration that an intruder can create without dropping anything malicious, both are invisible to reputation and signature controls by construction, and in both cases the only way to judge a given instance is against a register of what was supposed to be there. Organisations that have neither register discover during an incident that they cannot distinguish the attacker's ScreenConnect from the help desk's.
  Fields to collect, RMM and remote-access clients - for every installed or observed remote-access product - `product name`, `vendor`, `version`, `installation path`, `service name` and `ImagePath` (from 7045/4697), `scheduled task name` where persistence is by task (4698), `signing status and signer`, `instance ID or organisation ID`, `relay or server FQDN`, `destination port`, `install timestamp`, `installing process` and `installing user account`, the `host population` the product appears on (count and business unit), and the DISPOSITION - sanctioned or not - with a NAMED BUSINESS OWNER. Record whether the binary was portable or installed. Produce a per-product host count and a per-relay host count.
  Fields to collect, RMM servers - the same exercise server-side. Every on-premises or hosted RMM server the organisation runs, with version and patch level, exposure (internet-facing or not), administrator population, and whether its remote-execution and script-deployment features are logged centrally. An RMM SERVER compromise converts sanctioned tooling into estate-wide code execution in one step, which is why the register must cover the server tier and not only the clients - see [[H327]].
  Fields to collect, endpoint-security exclusions - for every exclusion - `exclusion type` (Path, Process, Extension, IpAddress), the `exact value`, `scope` (local registry hive, GPO, Intune/Configuration Manager policy), `who set it` (account and process, from Sysmon Event ID 13 or Defender 5007 old/new values), `when`, and the `justification` with an owner. Alongside each host record `tamper protection state`, `real-time protection state`, `cloud-delivered protection state`, `behaviour monitoring state`, and `notification suppression state`. Compute estate-wide coverage percentages for the three protection states - those percentages are a headline deliverable in their own right.
  Fields to collect, uninstall-key inventory - enumerate `HKLM\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Uninstall\` (and the WOW6432Node view) recording `DisplayName`, `Publisher`, `InstallDate`, `InstallLocation`, `UninstallString` and `SystemComponent`. Any entry with `SystemComponent=1` is hidden from Add/Remove Programs - Unit 42 observed exactly this used to conceal a Node.js installation dropped by an OfferLoader installer. Legitimate hidden entries exist (redistributables, update packages), so stack by publisher and treat unsigned or unfamiliar publishers with `SystemComponent=1` as the finding.
  Immediate flags, do not wait for the baseline to complete - (1) any exclusion at a drive root or of obviously excessive breadth - `C:\`, `C:\Users`, `%TEMP%`, `%APPDATA%`, `*`, or an extension exclusion for `.exe`/`.dll`; (2) any exclusion written by a process that is not the endpoint-management agent or a GPO/Intune policy application - especially `reg.exe`, `powershell.exe`, an MSI, or an installer running from a Downloads folder; (3) tamper protection disabled, or real-time protection disabled, on any host; (4) a SECOND instance of an RMM product that is already deployed in the estate, particularly a second client of the same product on one host; (5) any RMM client whose relay or server FQDN is not the organisation's own; (6) a ScreenConnect TRIAL relay (for example an `instance-<id>-relay[.]screenconnect[.]com` host) in an environment that holds a paid licence; (7) an RMM installer executed from a user's Downloads folder, or an RMM client installed by an interactive standard user rather than by the deployment system; (8) any executable running from a `...\ScreenConnect\Temp\` or `...\ConnectWiseControl\Temp\` path; (9) an uninstall entry with `SystemComponent=1` and an unsigned or unrecognised publisher; (10) outbound RMM traffic to an arbitrary IP on a non-standard port, such as the observed `144.172.115[.]59` on port 8041.
  Reference incident, Huntress, for calibration - two SOC incidents dated 25 and 31 August 2026. Victims were lured through a BROWSER-IN-THE-BROWSER page that draws fake browser chrome displaying the genuine-looking string `get.adobe.com`, then ran a fake Adobe Reader installer. The chain - user runs the fake installer from Downloads, rogue ScreenConnect client #1 installs as a Windows service, that client's session spawns `cmd.exe` and `curl` to fetch client #2, client #2 installs as a SECOND Windows service, and client #2 then runs evasion binaries. Huntress describe the command lines but do NOT print them - do not invent them, and do not build a filter on a string that was never published.
  Verbatim artefacts from that incident - installer URL `hosthiifran[.]screenconnect[.]com/Bin/ScreenConnect.ClientSetup.exe?e=Access&y=Guest&c=olojoaje&c=&c=&c=&c=&c=&c=&c=`. The `?e=Access&y=Guest&c=` parameter chain with SEVEN trailing empty `c=` values is a reusable ScreenConnect-abuse hunting string and is worth a standing query against proxy logs in its own right. Second-stage fetch `hxxps[://]wir[.]consultingics[.]com/Bin/ScreenConnect.ClientSetup.msi?e=Access&y=Guest&c=GOODVIBE&c=&c=&c=&c=&c=&c=&c=`, dropped as `patch.msi`. Rogue client instance IDs `9c1aea531ba4c511`, `7c1d255d0efefde6`, `d751818fd46e5ca9`, `c19e38a20f1ba492` - these appear in the service name and installation directory, which is why `instance ID` is a collected field. Relays `instance-uxh86b-relay[.]screenconnect[.]com` (a legitimate ScreenConnect TRIAL relay), `relay[.]goldenmelon[.]us`, `relay[.]illuminantgroup[.]net`, `scx[.]illuminantgroup[.]net`. C2 `144.172.115[.]59` on port 8041. Lures `adoube[.]vu/2a8ed9baefcd` and `selectstructure[.]com[.]au/freedom/adobedocument.html`; payload host `victory[.]mkc1[.]digitaloceanspaces[.]com/Adbrdb/AdbRdBkUpsStUp.msi`. Evasion binaries `HideCursor.exe` and `HideUL.exe`, executed from `C:\Users\<user>\Documents\ScreenConnect\Temp\`.
  Reference incident, Unit 42, for the exclusion axis - the OfferLoader installer script DISABLES WINDOWS DEFENDER PROTECTIONS, adds the ENTIRE `C:\` DRIVE as an exclusion, and SUPPRESSES SECURITY NOTIFICATIONS, then hides its Node.js component from Add/Remove Programs by setting `SystemComponent=1` in the uninstall registry key. A drive-root exclusion is the single most consequential configuration change an intruder can make on a Defender-protected host and it produces no detection of its own - it produces a 5007 configuration-change event that nobody is reading.
  Detection content to run alongside the collection - SigmaHQ `registry_set_defender_exclusions.yml` (`TargetObject|contains: '\Microsoft\Windows Defender\Exclusions'`), `win_security_windows_defender_exclusions_registry_modified.yml` (Security 4657, requires a registry SACL and audit policy), `win_security_windows_defender_exclusions_write_access.yml` (Security 4656/4663 write-access requests, tagged `attack.t1685`), `proc_creation_win_reg_defender_exclusion.yml` and `posh_ps_win_defender_exclusions_added.yml`. For ScreenConnect - `file_event_win_remote_access_tools_screenconnect_remote_file.yml` (files written to `\Documents\ConnectWiseControl\Temp\`) and `proc_creation_win_remote_access_tools_screenconnect_remote_execution.yml` (`ScreenConnect.ClientService.exe` spawning `cmd.exe` with `\TEMP\ScreenConnect\` in the command line). Every one of these documents "legitimate use" as its false positive, which is precisely the gap this baseline fills.
  Deliverable - four artefacts, all diffable, all re-snapshotted at the end of the window to produce the first diff. (1) A SANCTIONED-RMM REGISTER covering clients and servers, with product, version, instance ID, relay FQDN, host population, deployment mechanism and a named business owner per entry, and an explicit residual list of instances nobody claims. (2) An APPROVED-EXCLUSION REGISTER in which every exclusion carries a written justification, a scope, and an owner, with everything unjustified queued for removal. (3) A PROTECTION-COVERAGE STATEMENT - the percentage of the Windows estate with tamper protection enabled, with real-time protection enabled, and with cloud-delivered protection enabled, broken down by business unit, plus the list of hosts failing each. (4) A HIDDEN-SOFTWARE LIST - every uninstall entry with `SystemComponent=1` and an unrecognised publisher. Together these are what make [[H305]] and [[H327]] triageable, because both end at the same question this baseline answers in advance.

# B051

Two reports published weeks apart describe the same structural problem from opposite directions. Huntress worked two August 2026 SOC incidents in which a browser-in-the-browser page — fake browser chrome rendering the reassuring string `get.adobe.com` over an attacker page — led a user to run a counterfeit Adobe Reader installer. What arrived was not malware. It was ScreenConnect: a real, signed, commercially licensed remote-access product, installed as a Windows service, which then fetched and installed a second ScreenConnect client as a second service. Unit 42, analysing a pay-per-install network, described an installer that disabled Defender's protections, excluded the whole of `C:\` from scanning, suppressed security notifications, and hid its Node.js component from Add/Remove Programs by setting `SystemComponent=1`. Again, nothing that any signature or reputation engine is built to catch, because none of it is malicious code — it is configuration.

Both incidents terminate in a question a responder has to answer in minutes and usually cannot answer at all: is this instance supposed to be here? Is this ScreenConnect the help desk's or the intruder's? Is this exclusion the backup vendor's documented requirement or the thing that has been hiding a payload for three weeks? Without a register, the honest answer is a shrug, and the investigation stalls on an inventory question rather than a forensic one. This baseline builds both registers in advance, from telemetry most estates already collect, and produces a snapshot that can be diffed so that every subsequent run surfaces only what changed.

## Why

- **Legitimate software is the payload, so there is nothing to detect and everything to reconcile.** ScreenConnect is signed, reputable, widely deployed and behaves identically whether the operator is an MSP technician or an extortion crew. The only discriminator that exists is organisational knowledge — which instances, which relays, which owners — and that knowledge either has been written down in advance or it has not. No amount of detection engineering substitutes for it.
- **The relay is the durable discriminator and it survives every rotation.** Instance IDs, installer URLs, lure domains and C2 addresses all change between campaigns; Huntress already documented four distinct instance IDs across two incidents. What does not change is that a legitimate client talks to the organisation's own licensed server or its own vendor relay. An instance pointing somewhere else is not yours, whatever it is signed with — and a trial relay in a licensed environment is close to self-evidently wrong.
- **An exclusion list is security-relevant configuration that drifts constantly and is almost never reviewed.** Exclusions accumulate over years from vendor guidance, troubleshooting sessions and one-off waivers, nobody owns the aggregate, and the result is a control surface that the organisation has silently stopped trusting without deciding to. It is also the first thing an intruder edits, precisely because it is unwatched — and a drive-root exclusion produces only a 5007 configuration-change event that nobody is reading.
- **The two rogue clients in one incident are a warning about counting.** An estate that "has ScreenConnect" and therefore treats every ScreenConnect service as expected cannot see a second one arriving, which is exactly what happened here. Baselining at the level of PRODUCT is useless; the register has to be at the level of instance, relay and host, because the attacker's addition is a duplicate of something you already tolerate.
- **It produces risk findings that no alert would ever raise.** Shadow RMM bought by a business unit, an internet-facing RMM server nobody patches, exclusions for software that was decommissioned years ago, hosts with tamper protection quietly off — each is discovered by this exercise and by nothing else, each has an owner, and each is worth fixing whether or not the associated intrusion ever arrives.
